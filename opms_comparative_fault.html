Louisiana Federal Court · PACER Document 253 · March 2, 2026

OPMS Kratom: Can the Consumer Be Blamed?

A wrongful-death lawsuit asks whether a company can fault a consumer for using a product whose warnings are alleged to be inadequate.

← Back to OPMS Court Records

Kathleen Moller’s lawsuit concerns the death of her daughter, Harmony Moller, and OPMS kratom products. In this filing, she asks a federal judge to prevent the defendants from arguing that Harmony bears some responsibility for her own death.

At a glance

  • What is being decided: Whether comparative fault can be asserted against the consumer.
  • What the plaintiff says: The product warnings were inadequate, so the consumer should not be blamed for failing to anticipate undisclosed hazards.
  • What the coroner recorded: An anatomical coronary abnormality exacerbated by mitragynine use; manner of death: accident.
  • What remains contested: Whether kratom caused or contributed to the death and whether the warnings met legal requirements.
  • Evidence available: 20 original PACER PDFs, including labels, sworn testimony, the autopsy and competing expert reports.

What the original product labels show

The powder and capsule exhibits display ingredient information, mitragynine amounts, a 7-OH specification, and general directions to consult a healthcare professional. They also contain disclaimers placing responsibility for adverse consequences on the consumer.

The plaintiff argues that these statements did not adequately warn of the specific dangers at issue. The presence of a disclaimer does not, by itself, resolve whether a product was properly labeled under applicable law.

What the death investigation says

The St. Tammany Parish Coroner’s report identifies an abnormally high left coronary ostial takeoff exacerbated by mitragynine use as the cause of death and classifies the manner as accidental. It also reports pulmonary edema and congestion.

The defendants dispute the causal significance of kratom. Their expert reports offer competing interpretations of the medical and toxicological evidence. The coroner’s opinion is important evidence, but it does not settle the civil liability question.

What the sworn testimony addresses

Deposition excerpts question company representatives about warnings for products intended for human consumption, cardiovascular risk information, and the use of the phrase “botanical specimen.” The archived OPMS website also contains more extensive medical-condition and drug-interaction cautions.

For legislators, the question is practical: could a purchaser understand the product’s relevant risks from what appeared on the package at the time of sale?

The defense’s scientific position

The file includes reports from Drs. Jack Henningfield, Edward Boyer, Christopher McCurdy, Marilyn Huestis and Dennis Paustenbach. They address kratom’s pharmacology, toxicology, the interpretation of postmortem findings and alternative explanations for death.

These reports are defense-retained opinions. They should be read alongside the plaintiff’s evidence rather than treated as independent findings of the judge.

Why this matters to lawmakers

Product-safety laws depend on more than the existence of a warning. They depend on whether the warning conveys the relevant danger, whether its instructions are usable, and whether consumers can identify the responsible seller. This filing provides a concrete example of how those questions are litigated when a death is alleged to involve kratom leaf products.

The central dispute is not whether a warning existed. It is whether the warning adequately communicated the risk.
