The Ohio Files · Legislative Brief

What Lawmakers Need to Know

Four policy questions raised by the Ohio court record in Krazy Daze, Inc. et al. v. Ohio Board of Pharmacy.

The 60-Second Brief

1. Opioid pharmacology: Plaintiffs' expert described mitragynine as a partial mu-opioid receptor agonist and testified that concentrated kratom can become “more like morphine.”

2. Addiction and abuse: The trial court wrote about the addictive effects of mitragynine-related compounds and the possibility for kratom abuse, and denied the requested preliminary injunction.

3. Enforcement: The judge repeatedly asked how regulators can know whether a finished beverage contains leaf, extract, added mitragynine or stronger alkaloids without relying on labels and laboratory analysis.

4. Scale: Botanic Tonics' own exhibit described more than 1,600 retailers, millions of dollars in Feel Free sales, and an estimated $87.5 million annual Ohio natural-kratom market.

Issue 1

Is This an Opioid-Regulation Question?

The plaintiff-side expert tied mitragynine to the mu-opioid receptor and the reward pathway, described it as a partial agonist, and said concentration can make the pharmacology more like morphine.

Legislative question: Should a commercially sold mu-opioid agonist be regulated as an ordinary botanical simply because its starting material is a leaf?

Issue 2

What Did the Court Conclude About Public Interest?

The court expressly referenced addictive effects and the possibility for abuse and said plaintiffs' expert did not convince it that the public interest favored the requested injunction.

Legislative question: What evidence would justify assuming a broad retail carve-out is safe?

Issue 3

Can the State Identify the Legal Product at the Shelf?

The hearing exposes the difficulty of telling leaf from extract or detecting added alkaloids by appearance. The legal answer may depend on chemistry that requires a laboratory.

Legislative question: Does the proposed law come with a funded testing and enforcement system capable of distinguishing the categories it creates?

Issue 4

Can That System Work at Commercial Scale?

The manufacturer presented a mass-retail market with more than 1,600 retailers and millions in sales. A chemistry-dependent rule must work across that entire network.

Legislative question: Who tests, who pays, who removes failures, and how fast?

Questions to Ask Before Voting on Kratom Legislation

  1. Does the bill treat all products labeled “kratom” as though they have the same pharmacology?
  2. How does the law distinguish whole leaf, extract, concentrate, isolated alkaloids and products with added alkaloids?
  3. What validated analytical method will the state use?
  4. What limit of quantitation triggers enforcement?
  5. Can a retailer or officer determine legal status without a laboratory test?
  6. Who samples products and maintains chain of custody?
  7. Who pays for routine testing across thousands of retail locations?
  8. How does the bill address mitragynine's mu-opioid activity and abuse potential?
  9. What evidence establishes a proposed serving size or alkaloid ceiling as safe for long-term use?
  10. What happens immediately when a product fails?
