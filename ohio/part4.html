The Ohio Files · Part 4

From Leaf to Mass Market

Botanic Tonics’ own Ohio court exhibit describes Feel Free as a mass-market product sold through more than 1,600 retailers and presents multi-million-dollar sales and a large estimated Ohio kratom market.

Krazy Daze, Inc. et al. v. Ohio Board of Pharmacy · Case No. 26 CV 004790

This Is Not a Niche Herbal Market

Botanic Tonics' own “Market Landscape – Ohio” slide told the court that Feel Free was sold in more than 1,600 retailers, that 2025 Feel Free sales were $4.8 million, and that lifetime sales exceeded $13 million.

The same slide estimated the natural-kratom market in Ohio at approximately $87.5 million per year, applying an industry estimate to Ohio's share of the national population.

Evidence boundary: The $87.5 million figure is an estimate presented in Botanic Tonics' exhibit, not an audited statewide sales total.

The Slide Uses Convenience-Retail Branding

The “Market Landscape – Ohio” exhibit prominently displays the logos of Circle K and Murphy USA while discussing Feel Free's retail reach.

The significance is not that every location of those chains necessarily carried every kratom product. The significance is the mass-retail context the manufacturer itself chose to show the court.

The modern policy problem is not confined to specialty herb shops. The record describes a product category sold through ordinary retail channels.

Why Scale Changes the Enforcement Equation

A regulatory system that depends on laboratory chemistry may be manageable for a small number of licensed manufacturers. It becomes much more complicated when products are distributed through a broad network of convenience and general retail outlets.

At that scale, lawmakers must decide who is responsible for verifying composition: the manufacturer, distributor, retailer, health department, pharmacy board, agriculture department, local police — or some combination of all of them.

Botanic Tonics' Own Safety Framework Depends on Controls

The company's slide deck told the court that safety rests on multiple layers: manufacturing specifications, labels, post-market surveillance, validated analytical methods, toxicology, clinical studies and third-party verification.

That presentation is useful because it implicitly acknowledges that “natural” is not itself a quality-control system. Commercial safety depends on testing and process controls.

The Synthetic / Natural Distinction Is Also a Market Strategy

Botanic Tonics' presentation states that the company supports natural kratom leaf with a specified composition while saying it is not aware of a safety basis for synthetic kratom alkaloids or ingredients modified by addition of synthetic alkaloids.

That is a policy position, not a neutral scientific finding. But when paired with the testimony in Part I, it reveals the industry's preferred dividing line: preserve the leaf-based commercial market while excluding more concentrated or synthetic competitors.

For lawmakers, the question is whether that dividing line is scientifically meaningful, legally durable and practically enforceable at thousands of retail points.

What the Record Does — and Does Not — Establish

  • It does show that Botanic Tonics presented Feel Free as a product sold through more than 1,600 retailers.
  • It does show multi-million-dollar sales figures in the company's court exhibit.
  • It does show that plaintiffs' safety case depends on manufacturing, testing, labeling and surveillance controls.
  • It does not establish that every retailer sold an illegal or unsafe product.
  • It does not establish that every kratom product has the same chemical composition or risk.
  • It does establish that any regulatory system must operate at commercial scale, not merely theoretical scale.

Why This Matters for Lawmakers

When a market is broad, fast-moving and chemically heterogeneous, the administrative cost of distinguishing legal from illegal products becomes part of the safety question.

A standard is only as protective as the state's ability to test it, police it and remove noncompliant products before consumers buy them.
