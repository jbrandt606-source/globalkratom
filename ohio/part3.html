The Ohio Files · Part 3

What’s Actually in the Bottle?

The Ohio hearing exposes a basic enforcement problem: when kratom is sold as a finished beverage, how can a regulator know whether it contains leaf, extract, added mitragynine, 7-OH or another alkaloid without laboratory testing?

Krazy Daze, Inc. et al. v. Ohio Board of Pharmacy · Case No. 26 CV 004790
Leaf?Cannot be confirmed by appearance
Extract?Manufacturing history matters
Added alkaloids?Not visible on inspection
Lab testMay decide legal status

The Judge Asked the Right Regulatory Question

During the hearing, the court repeatedly pushed past labels and asked how anyone would know what was actually inside a commercial kratom beverage.

The judge asked how officials could determine whether ingredients came only from leaf and powder, whether a beverage had been enhanced with extra mitragynine, or whether it contained something stronger such as 7-OH.

That is the enforcement problem in one sentence: the legal category may depend on chemistry that cannot be seen at the shelf.

A Finished Beverage Does Not Reveal Its Manufacturing History

One of the plaintiffs' core arguments was that a product made from ground leaf and water should retain the legal status of “vegetative” kratom. But the court asked what happens when the material is sold in a beverage and other ingredients are present.

The judge specifically questioned how an officer could know whether something had been extracted, added or concentrated — and noted that regulators might be relying on a manufacturer's label.

The Expert's Own Distinction Depends on Processing

Verkerk's testimony distinguished traditional leaf from extracts and isolated alkaloids. He described extraction as concentrating the components of concern and described oxidation routes that can produce more potent opioid-active compounds.

If the risk distinction depends on whether the material was extracted, concentrated, oxidized or supplemented, then the regulator needs evidence of process and composition — not just a package description.

Selective Bans Require Selective Testing

Ohio's policy attempted to distinguish mitragynine in vegetative form from other mitragynine-related compounds. That may be clear on paper. At retail, however, a selective system creates operational questions:

  • Who samples the product?
  • What method distinguishes leaf-derived alkaloids from added alkaloids?
  • What limit of quantitation is required?
  • How is chain of custody maintained?
  • How quickly can the state test a convenience-store product?
  • Who pays for routine testing?
  • What happens while results are pending?
  • How does an officer identify an illegal product before laboratory confirmation?

Labeling Helps — But It Does Not Answer the Judge's Question

Botanic Tonics told the court that labeling is crucial to safe botanical-product regulation. That is a reasonable proposition as far as it goes. But a label is still a manufacturer's representation.

The judge's concern was deeper: how do regulators know whether the contents match the representation and whether something has been added that changes the legal or pharmacological category?

A legal distinction that cannot be verified outside a laboratory is not a simple retail rule. It is a statewide chemical-enforcement program.

Why This Supports a Broader Sales-Ban Argument

The Ohio record does not prove that every kratom product presents identical risk. In fact, plaintiffs' expert argues strongly that traditional leaf differs from concentrated products.

But that distinction creates its own policy problem: if officials cannot reliably identify which category a finished product belongs to at the point of sale, legislators must decide whether the state has the money, laboratories, inspectors and enforcement machinery to operate a product-by-product chemical regime.

The policy choice is not merely “ban versus regulate.” It is also whether the proposed regulation can actually be verified and enforced in the real retail market.
