FEDERAL LEGISLATION · S. 5383 · OCTOBER 2026

End Gas Station Heroin Act. But Not Gas Station Kratom.

A bill aimed at synthetic opioids preserves a federal carve-out for qualifying botanical kratom products. Here is what the language actually does, and what it leaves untouched.

THE SHORT VERSION

What this bill would actually do

S. 5383 would add a targeted federal Schedule I prohibition for 7-hydroxymitragynine (7-OH) and mitragynine pseudoindoxyl, with explicit exemptions for qualifying naturally occurring amounts in kratom products. It would also create a separate commercial-enforcement mechanism for certain emerging synthetic opioids.

1 mg/gMaximum combined 7-OH and pseudoindoxyl in qualifying solid products, subject to other conditions
1 mg/mLMaximum combined amount in qualifying liquids, subject to other conditions
1%Combined amount cannot exceed 1 part per 100 parts mitragynine by mass
0Provisions scheduling mitragynine itself or the kratom plant as a whole

These are cumulative requirements, not alternative safe harbors. The text says the 7-OH and pseudoindoxyl limits apply individually or in combination; the mass ratio also must be met. They are legal thresholds, not scientific findings of safety.

Where does the kratom industry stand?

The American Kratom Association publicly endorsed the bill. In a September 26, 2026 statement titled “AKA Strongly Supports Connor's End Gas Station Heroin Act,” the organization urged Congress to advance the legislation and specifically praised its focus on synthetic 7-OH, pseudoindoxyl, MGM-15 and MGM-16 products.

The policy alignment is plain: The bill targets certain manufactured kratom-derived substances while leaving qualifying botanical kratom outside the new scheduling language. That is consistent with the AKA's longstanding distinction between botanical kratom and high-potency manufactured products.

What this does not prove: Public endorsement is evidence of industry advocacy and alignment, not proof that the AKA wrote the bill, directed its sponsors or exchanged anything of value. Such claims would require drafting records, correspondence, lobbying disclosures or other documentary evidence.

What happens to botanical kratom?

Whole leaf and plain powderNot scheduled as such by this bill. Naturally occurring 7-OH and pseudoindoxyl may qualify for the exemption if every numerical condition is met.
CapsulesCapsulated kratom falls under the solid-product limits. The exemption is conditional, not blanket approval.
Liquid kratom productsThe bill specifies a 1 mg/mL limit and a separate 1% relative-to-mitragynine requirement. A product also must meet the natural-occurrence restriction.
Concentrated or chemically converted products7-OH or pseudoindoxyl that has been synthesized, converted, isolated, enriched or concentrated through manufacturing does not qualify for the Section 2 exemption.
The central omission: The bill does not schedule mitragynine, the principal alkaloid in kratom leaf, or Mitragyna speciosa itself. It therefore does not create a nationwide prohibition on ordinary botanical kratom. Other federal and state laws may still apply.

The fine print: what the bill actually covers

01

Section 2: two named compounds

Adds 7-OH and mitragynine pseudoindoxyl and specified chemical forms to Schedule I, but expressly excludes qualifying naturally occurring quantities. Manufactured, enriched, isolated or chemically converted material does not get that exemption.

02

Section 3: a narrower commercial enforcement tool

Applies to certain synthetic or semisynthetic opioid receptor agonists that satisfy multiple criteria, including scientifically reliable evidence of potency greater than morphine under the bill's tests. It targets knowing or intentional commercial conduct, not simple personal possession under this section.

03

Botanical constituents have their own exclusion

Section 3(g)(3) excludes naturally occurring botanical constituents and those derived by brewing or extraction unless intentionally isolated, enriched, concentrated, chemically converted or added to a product for human consumption. That is not an unconditional exemption for every extract.

04

MGM-15 and MGM-16: a drafting question

The bill's long title says it will schedule MGM-15 and MGM-16. But the operative text supplied does not name them in the Schedule I amendment. They might fall within Section 3 if all its criteria are proved; the title alone does not schedule them.

DOCUMENTED BEFORE THE SYNTHETIC-7-OH BOOM

The hospitalizations and deaths did not start in 2024

Years before today's concentrated kratom-derived products became prominent, poison centers and medical examiners were already recording serious kratom-associated outcomes. The figures below describe reported exposures and death investigations, not proof that every event involved pure botanical leaf or that kratom was the sole cause.

The warning signs extended to newborns

Physicians had published neonatal withdrawal cases associated with maternal kratom use by 2018, well before the current wave of highly concentrated products. Such reports demonstrate that dependence-related concerns were not invented in 2024. 2018 Pediatrics case report ↗ 2018 online case report ↗

The question for Congress: If botanical kratom is left outside the bill's scheduling provisions, how does the legislation address the harms that were already being reported years before the newer products reached the market?

Interpretation matters: surveillance counts can include extracts, adulterated products and co-ingestants; “sole cause” refers to a medical examiner's documented determination in the reviewed cases, not proof of chemical purity. FDA adverse-event reports cannot establish incidence rates or independently prove causation.

Five questions Congress should answer

  • Why exempt the source plant? If the concern includes opioid-active kratom constituents, why leave mitragynine and ordinary botanical kratom outside the bill's scheduling provisions?
  • Why these thresholds? What evidence establishes that 1 mg/g, 1 mg/mL and a 1% mitragynine ratio are appropriate legal boundaries?
  • Who will test? How will regulators distinguish naturally occurring amounts from isolated, enriched or converted compounds in finished products?
  • What about new analogs? Will the potency and evidentiary tests in Section 3 allow timely enforcement against rapidly changing substances?
  • Why the title-text mismatch? Why do MGM-15 and MGM-16 appear in the bill's stated purpose but not as named compounds in its operative Schedule I amendment?
Bottom line: Congress can take action against manufactured kratom-derived opioids without pretending that botanical kratom has a clean record. This bill draws a legal boundary around certain molecules. It does not settle the broader public-health question posed by the plant from which they originate.
