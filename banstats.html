They Banned Kratom. The Disaster Never Came.

A decade of real-world data from states that prohibited kratom tests a familiar prediction: that removing legal kratom would drive people toward more dangerous drugs and produce broad public health harm.

10-Second Summary

  • Arkansas overdose mortality: 13.9 deaths per 100,000 in 2024, compared with 23.1 nationally.
  • Arkansas overdose deaths: down about 25% from 517 in 2023 to 387 in 2024 while kratom remained prohibited.
  • Violent crime: lower in 2025 than 2017 in four of the six states examined, including declines of about 24% in Alabama, 23% in Indiana, 24% in Wisconsin, and 35% in Rhode Island.
  • Property crime: lower in all six states from 2017 to 2025. Five states declined roughly 43% to 46%.

What this means: These figures do not prove that kratom prohibition caused any improvement. They do show that long-standing prohibition is compatible with falling overdose deaths and substantial crime declines, contrary to a simple prediction of inevitable public-health or social deterioration.

The Central Question

The claim is testable

Opponents of prohibition argue that banning kratom will push consumers toward fentanyl and other dangerous opioids. Five states have maintained kratom prohibitions for roughly a decade or longer. Does the overdose data show that predicted effect? No consistent pattern appears.

  • Arkansas: 13.9 overdose deaths per 100,000 in 2024, compared with 23.1 nationally.
  • Wisconsin: 19.8 per 100,000, also below the national rate.
  • Vermont: 33.6 per 100,000, substantially above the national rate.

Same broad policy category. Very different outcomes. That does not prove prohibition works. It does undermine a categorical claim that prohibition itself predictably produces an overdose catastrophe.

Bottom Line

States that prohibited kratom roughly a decade ago do not show a consistent pattern of increased overdose mortality, crime, or broader social deterioration. Some perform better than national averages and some worse. These comparisons do not prove that prohibition caused any outcome. They do challenge claims that prohibition itself predictably produces a public-health catastrophe.

The Prediction Can Be Tested

When states consider prohibiting kratom, they often hear a warning: take kratom away and people will return to heroin, fentanyl, or other dangerous opioids. Overdoses will rise. Public health will suffer.

That is a testable claim.

Alabama, Arkansas, Indiana, Wisconsin, and Vermont have maintained long-standing prohibitions. Rhode Island also prohibited kratom for years before changing course toward a regulated legal framework.

The question is not whether prohibition made these states better. The question is whether the predicted catastrophe actually appeared.

These comparisons do not establish causation. Fentanyl penetration, treatment access, naloxone distribution, demographics, economic conditions, COVID-19, and many other factors affect state outcomes.

Arkansas Is the Awkward Case for the Catastrophe Argument

Arkansas prohibited kratom in 2016. In 2024, its age-adjusted all-drug overdose mortality rate was 13.9 per 100,000, well below the national rate of 23.1.

Arkansas recorded 517 overdose deaths in 2023 and 387 in 2024.

Arkansas test case

  • Kratom prohibited since 2016
  • 2024 overdose rate: 13.9 per 100,000
  • U.S. rate: 23.1 per 100,000
  • 2023 overdose deaths: 517
  • 2024 overdose deaths: 387
  • One-year change: about 25% lower

This does not mean Arkansas reduced overdoses because it banned kratom. It does demonstrate that a state can maintain kratom prohibition and still achieve a large reduction in overdose deaths.

CDC/NCHS final overdose counts and CDC final 2024 state overdose rates.

Alabama Shows Improvement Without Legal Kratom

Alabama's overdose history is mixed, and it should be presented that way. Final overdose deaths fell from 1,598 in 2023 to 1,175 in 2024, about a 26% decline, although the 2024 age-adjusted rate of 25.0 remained above the national rate.

Other public health measures improved too. Alabama's 2024 infant mortality rate was 7.1 deaths per 1,000 live births. The Alabama Department of Public Health reports that the state reached its lowest recorded infant mortality rate, 6.7, in 2022.

Wisconsin Has Lived Without Legal Kratom for More Than a Decade

Wisconsin's prohibition dates to 2014. Its 2024 age-adjusted all-drug overdose mortality rate was 19.8 per 100,000, below the national rate of 23.1. Final 2024 overdose deaths totaled 1,164.

Wisconsin experienced the fentanyl crisis. The point is not that prohibition protected it. The point is that more than a decade without a legal kratom market has not produced a unique or obvious overdose catastrophe.

Source

CDC/NCHS Drug Overdose Mortality and CDC final overdose counts.

Vermont Counts Too

A credible comparison cannot discard the state with inconvenient numbers. Vermont's 2024 overdose mortality rate was 33.6 per 100,000, well above the national rate, with 205 final overdose deaths.

If Vermont's high rate is offered as proof that prohibition causes overdose deaths, Arkansas immediately creates a problem for that argument. Arkansas has the same broad prohibition policy category and a rate of 13.9.

Neither state proves causation. Together, they show why simplistic state-level causal claims are weak.

Source

CDC/NCHS Drug Overdose Mortality.

Indiana Belongs in the Analysis

Indiana's 2024 overdose mortality rate was 25.7 per 100,000, above the national rate. Final overdose deaths totaled 1,688.

Indiana belongs here precisely because the number is not especially favorable. A serious policy review should include all of the long-standing prohibition states, not just the ones that make the preferred argument look good.

CDC/NCHS Drug Overdose Mortality and CDC final overdose counts.

What This Data Shows, and What It Does Not

What the Data Show

  • Long-standing prohibition states do not exhibit a consistent overdose pattern.
  • Some prohibition states are below the national overdose rate.
  • Some are above it.
  • Large overdose declines have occurred while prohibition remained in effect.
  • States can improve unrelated health and education measures without a legal kratom market.

What the Data Do Not Show

  • That banning kratom caused overdose rates to fall.
  • That prohibition caused improvements in education or infant mortality.
  • That Vermont's high overdose rate was caused by its kratom ban.
  • That state-level comparisons establish individual-level causation.

Rhode Island Gives Us a Different Test

Rhode Island later changed course from prohibition toward a regulated legal framework, so it should not be presented as a permanently banned state.

Instead, it can become a prospective policy test. If regulated legal access produces the public health benefits advocates predict, those benefits should eventually be measurable in overdose mortality, poison-center calls, emergency-department visits, treatment data, and kratom-related adverse events.

If regulated legal access produces the public health benefits advocates predict, Rhode Island should eventually provide measurable evidence of them.

Current comparison source

CDC reports 333 final overdose deaths in Rhode Island in 2024. See CDC/NCHS Vital Statistics Rapid Release.

Broader Social Indicators

Overdose mortality is the most directly relevant measure for claims that prohibition drives people toward more dangerous opioids. Crime, education, infant mortality, employment, poverty, and other social indicators are included only as broader context. They are not presented as outcomes caused by kratom policy.

These measures answer a narrower question: did states without legal kratom become unable to improve on ordinary measures of public health and social welfare? The record does not show such a general collapse.

Other Metrics Matter Too

The overdose question is the most directly relevant to claims that prohibition sends people toward more dangerous opioids. Broader measures are useful for a different reason. They test claims that states cannot function or improve without legal access to kratom.

  • Infant mortality: Alabama reached a record low of 6.7 deaths per 1,000 live births in 2022 and stood at 7.1 in 2024.
  • Education: Alabama's average NAEP fourth-grade mathematics score rose from 230 in 2019 to 236 in 2024, essentially level with the 2024 national public-school average of 237.
  • Crime: FBI Crime Data Explorer trends above show substantial declines in violent crime in several states and large property-crime declines in five of the six states examined.
  • Economic and social measures: employment, poverty, household income, teen births, and other indicators can provide additional context, but each has many causes and should be interpreted separately.

None of these improvements can be attributed to kratom prohibition. The narrower point is that they occurred without a legal kratom market. Prohibition did not prevent states from making progress on unrelated measures of public health, education, crime, and social welfare.

The Question the Data Should Answer

When the claim is made that banning kratom will send consumers to fentanyl, the next question should be simple: what does the population-level evidence show?

Which prohibition state demonstrates that effect?

  • Arkansas is far below the national overdose rate.
  • Wisconsin is below it.
  • Alabama and Indiana are somewhat above it.
  • Vermont is substantially above it.

There is no consistent prohibition pattern.

That does not prove bans reduce overdoses. It means the public should not be told that bans increase them without evidence either.

A prediction repeated often is still a prediction. The states that already banned kratom give us something better: data.
