FACTOR EIGHT / History & precedent

A law designed to act before the final product.

The immediate-precursor authority dates to 1970. DEA has used it to regulate manufacturing inputs separately from the ordinary abuse-potential analysis.

1970: Congress created a separate route.

The Controlled Substances Act was enacted as Title II of Public Law 91-513 on October 27, 1970. Its precursor authority was part of the original scheme—not a later invention created for kratom. The present § 811(e) derives from section 201 of that Act. [L2 · source credit and editorial notes]

In its 1979 phenylacetone order, DEA explained the reason for the separate mechanism: a chemical could remain uncontrolled even though it stood immediately before the manufacture of a controlled drug. The agency described the authority as an alternative to the more extensive procedures used for ordinary drug scheduling. This is DEA’s published explanation of the provision, not a claim that every precursor petition must be granted. [L6 · 44 FR 71822]

1979–1980: the phenylacetone order.

On December 12, 1979, DEA published an order placing phenylacetone, also called P2P, in Schedule II as an immediate precursor of amphetamine and methamphetamine. The main control date was February 11, 1980, with specified compliance dates. The order separately stated each of the three required findings. [L6 · 44 FR 71822–71824]

Its necessity explanation connected chemical access to practical controls: registration, records, security and import requirements. DEA argued that requiring these safeguards would restrict previously unhindered transactions. The record also included substantial actual clandestine-manufacturing evidence and considered effects on legitimate industry. This was not a precedent based on a hypothetical reaction alone. [L6]

2010: ANPP and the fentanyl manufacturing input.

DEA’s June 29, 2010 final rule designated ANPP an immediate precursor of fentanyl and placed it in Schedule II, effective August 30, 2010. DEA expressly used § 811(e), rather than requiring the ordinary scheduling findings for ANPP itself. [L7 · 75 FR 37295–37299]

The useful comparison is the agency’s method: identify the immediate chemical input, establish its role in the controlled product’s manufacture, and explain how controlling the input addresses diversion. The schedule was II because fentanyl was in II. This is not a Schedule I precedent.

2020: norfentanyl and the preventive rationale.

DEA’s April 17, 2020 final rule designated norfentanyl as an immediate precursor and placed it in Schedule II, effective May 18, 2020. The agency discussed alternative manufacturing routes, supplier availability and route-specific forensic evidence. [L8 · 85 FR 21320–21321]

For necessity, DEA also reasoned prospectively: precursor control would add safeguards against diversion and prevent access to an unregulated intermediate. The agency did not claim that control of one input would eliminate every route to fentanyl. But its record was not identical to mitragynine’s: DEA reported no other identified legitimate use for norfentanyl, whereas the supplied mitragynine record includes botanical consumption and research uses. [L8 · 85 FR 21321–21322]

What these precedents support.

Supported lessonApplication here
Separate precursor authority is established.Use § 811(e) and § 802(23), not the slogan “Factor 8 alone” as a substitute for the legal findings.
Necessity is a practical regulatory argument.Explain registration, security, records and the prevention of uncontrolled acquisition before conversion.
Alternative routes do not automatically end the inquiry.Address the MP total-synthesis literature and explain why restricting a documented route can still limit manufacture.
The precedents do not guarantee identical treatment.Acknowledge legitimate uses, botanical preparations, target scheduling and the breadth of the requested mitragynine control.

The present proposal uses the same legal structure.

The petition asks DEA to make affirmative findings on the principal compound, the immediate intermediary, and the necessity of control. It does not ask DEA to assume that all three have already been settled. The preventive argument is grounded in the statutory text; the manufacturing argument is grounded in the supplied scientific and commercial record. [L1]
