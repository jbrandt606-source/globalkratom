What the laboratory record can establish.

Reported composition supports the record; a COA is not a substitute for manufacturing provenance.

Immediate-precursor petition recordLegal review: November 4, 2026Proposed findings, not a DEA determination

A laboratory result answers a composition question.

A certificate of analysis describes the submitted sample and reported analytes under the stated method. It does not, by itself, establish who manufactured the sample, what input was used, or whether a specific starting-material lot became a finished product. The supplied COA index identifies fifteen distinct report records and no authenticated matched input/output pair. [E73]

The retained report for MIT extract powder is relevant to the availability of mitragynine-containing material. Do not turn it into proof that the same lot was used in a specific 7-OH product. [E70 · PDF p. 1]

The analytical papers add context and limits.

E48PDF pp. 5–9

Commercial-product patterns

The authors’ analysis supports discussion of semisynthetic formulations in the sampled products. Sample-selection limits and possible analytical or stability artifacts remain relevant. [E48 · PDF pp. 5–9]

E32Analytical findings and conclusion

Correct identity matters

Related compounds can be difficult to distinguish under insufficiently specific methods. Evaluate the actual laboratory method rather than treating a shared analytical signal as definitive identification. [E32 · Analytical findings and conclusion]

How the laboratory material is used.

The petition uses these records as corroborating evidence of material composition, product availability and analytical questions. Its principal manufacturing argument relies on preparative chemistry and the commercial account. Its necessity argument is a separate prospective inference about input control.
