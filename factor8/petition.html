I. Petition and relief requested

To the Administrator, Drug Enforcement Administration:

I submit this petition in my individual capacity as a Virginia-licensed hospital pharmacist. I request that DEA designate mitragynine as an immediate precursor of 7-hydroxymitragynine (7-OH), within the scope of the effective federal Schedule I listing of above-threshold 7-OH, under 21 U.S.C. § 802(23), and place mitragynine and its salts in Schedule I under 21 U.S.C. § 811(e) and 21 CFR § 1308.47. The requested listing includes materials, compounds, mixtures and preparations containing mitragynine, including mitragynine-containing botanical material. [L1] [L2] [L3]

This petition is prepared for submission following the effective scheduling of the target. It supplies the proposed rule and statement of grounds contemplated by 21 CFR § 1308.43(b). My identification, correspondence address, filing date and signature belong in the accompanying private filing cover letter. [L4]

The requested determination rests on three propositions: mitragynine is the principal compound used in the documented manufacture of 7-OH; it is an immediate chemical intermediary used or likely to be used in that manufacture; and controlling its availability is necessary to prevent, curtail or limit manufacture of the controlled target. Each proposition is addressed separately below. I request Schedule I placement as an exercise of DEA’s discretion, not as an automatic consequence of the target’s scheduling.

7-OH is the sole target relied upon for this petition’s immediate-precursor designation. Evidence concerning mitragynine pseudoindoxyl (MP) and other derivatives is retained where it illuminates the record, alternative pathways or analytical specificity. Relief does not depend on establishing a separate mitragynine-to-MP precursor relationship.

II. The controlled target, threshold and effective-order condition

DEA’s October 14, 2026 notice, Docket DEA-1570, 91 FR 65370–65381, announces intended temporary Schedule I control of 7-OH and MP above specified thresholds. It states that a temporary scheduling order will be published on or after November 13, 2026. The notice is the basis for preparing this petition in anticipation of that action; it is not itself treated as the effective order. [L9]

Effective-order record — to be completed for filing

The final filing must identify the issued temporary scheduling order, its Federal Register citation, publication and effective dates, exact CFR entry, controlling threshold language and exceptions. As prepared on November 12, 2026, those future order details are not supplied or assumed. The petition requests precursor control only once, and to the extent that, the relevant 7-OH target is actually in Schedule I.

For purposes of this anticipatory record, the October notice identifies two alternative triggers. Either trigger would bring the specified material within the proposed coverage:

These are the notice’s proposed criteria, subject to the issued order. The solid concentration test uses the mass of the material, not a manufacturer-selected serving size. For material containing 7-OH alone, the ratio’s denominator is the mass of 7-OH. For mixed 7-OH/MP preparations, the controlling order’s combined-substance language must be applied. No target coverage is inferred from a product name alone. [L9]

The target’s concentration limit and the proposed precursor’s scope answer different questions. The first identifies the 7-OH material already controlled; the second concerns access to the molecule used to manufacture that material. Section 811(e) addresses the precursor’s schedule, while § 802(23) supplies the precursor findings. Neither provision expressly imports a target-product concentration threshold into every precursor listing. I therefore request a separate, reasoned scope determination for mitragynine, with the botanical consequences expressly considered in Section VIII. [L1] [L2]

A temporary Schedule I listing is still inclusion in a CSA schedule: § 802(6) defines controlled substance by inclusion in Schedules I–V, and § 811(e) does not expressly require that the target’s listing be permanent. That text supports considering precursor relief while the target’s temporary listing is effective. DEA should address this statutory basis expressly, including the effect of any later expiration, withdrawal, replacement or change in the target’s scope. This petition does not assume that its premise survives such a change without legal analysis. [L1] [L2]

IV. Legal authority and the applicable findings

Factor 8, 21 U.S.C. § 811(c)(8), concerns whether a substance is an immediate precursor of an already controlled substance. The separate operative authority is § 811(e), implemented by 21 CFR § 1308.47. It permits precursor placement without the ordinary findings specified in §§ 811(a) and 812(b) and without the procedures specified in §§ 811(a) and (b), while preserving the need to establish the immediate-precursor definition. This request invokes that authority through a petition in the form contemplated by § 1308.43(b). [L2] [L3] [L4]

Under § 802(23), DEA must find and designate a substance that:

  1. is the principal compound used, or produced primarily for use, in manufacturing a controlled substance;
  2. is an immediate chemical intermediary used or likely to be used in that manufacture; and
  3. requires control to prevent, curtail or limit that manufacture.

The first prong’s alternatives matter. This petition relies on the principal compound used. It does not assert that most mitragynine worldwide is produced for conversion into 7-OH. Independent consumer uses therefore do not alone resolve Prong A against the petition. They remain relevant to necessity, scope and regulatory consequences. Conversely, merely showing that a compound can be used does not dispense with the principal-compound finding; the preparative and commercial record must support it. [L1]

Under § 802(15), manufacture includes production, preparation, processing, extraction, chemical synthesis and combinations of extraction and synthesis. Botanical origin does not exclude a process from this definition. The relevant subject here is industrial or laboratory production of 7-OH. Ingestion and subsequent metabolism are not the manufacturing premise on which relief is requested. [L1]

DEA precedent supplies a mechanism, not a substitute for this record. DEA designated ANPP in 2010 and norfentanyl in 2020 as immediate precursors of fentanyl and placed them in Schedule II. The rules connect a direct manufacturing relationship to diversion-prevention controls. In the norfentanyl rule, DEA explained the value of safeguards against theft and uncontrolled supply through commercial channels before conversion. [L7] [L8]

Those rules also identify a material difference: DEA found the fentanyl precursors were produced primarily for fentanyl manufacture and identified no other legitimate industrial uses. Mitragynine has a substantial consumer market and research uses. The petition therefore relies on the alternative principal-compound branch and supplies an explicit necessity and scope analysis instead of treating the precedents as factually identical. Their Schedule II outcomes are not represented as Schedule I precedents. The permissible schedule here follows § 811(e) and the effective Schedule I target listing.

The requested schedule is Schedule I because the petition seeks controls commensurate with the controlled target, including restrictions on diversion of its immediate manufacturing input. Section 811(e) permits a schedule with a higher numerical designation, so DEA retains discretion. If DEA finds a different permissible schedule better supported, I request a reasoned partial grant rather than treating disagreement over schedule as fatal to the immediate-precursor designation. [L2] [L3]

V. Prong A: the principal compound used in manufacture

A. Direct preparative evidence identifies mitragynine

US10961244B2, Example 19, reports preparation and characterization of ordinary 7-OH from mitragynine. Kures’ WO2022234524A1, Example 1, identifies mitragynine recovered from powdered kratom leaves and reports 7-OH preparations from that mitragynine, including a larger-scale preparation. These passages identify mitragynine as the substance transformed into 7-OH, rather than a solvent, catalyst or incidental constituent. [E04 · Example 19; PDF pp. 61–62] [E16 · ¶¶ 301–303; PDF pp. 45–47]

Sakamoto, Kitajima and Ishikawa’s 2022 peer-reviewed paper independently reports synthesis of mitragynine and preparation of 7-OH through direct C-7 oxidation of that molecule. Its total-synthesis approach supplies mitragynine before making 7-OH; it does not establish that the mitragynine molecule is bypassed merely because harvested leaf is bypassed. This strengthens both the input finding and a source-neutral regulatory description of mitragynine. [E31 · PDF pp. 3, 6–7]

B. Commercial sources connect the chemistry to production

American Shaman’s July 30, 2026 publication describes a documentary filmed at its Kansas City production facility. The company account describes processing kratom leaf extract through oxidation, analysis and purification to produce 7-OH, including industrial equipment. This is a manufacturer’s account of production, rather than a retail label stating only the finished ingredient. [E18 · PDF pp. 6–7]

Stephen Vincent Sanders II’s affidavit identifies the companies included in his use of “American Shaman,” describes separately formulated products derived from kratom, states that 7-OH sales began in late 2023, and describes investment, manufacturing personnel and batch-release testing. The sworn statements support an established commercial enterprise and a practical market for production. They do not identify the mitragynine input for a particular batch. The affidavit’s safety and legality assertions remain the affiant’s positions. [E23 · ¶¶ 2, 10–17; PDF pp. 2, 4–5]

The FDA correspondence provides an additional contemporaneous connection. In its December 3, 2025 request to Shaman Botanicals, FDA states: “Your notification indicates that your 7-OH is manufactured from kratom.” FDA then requests raw-material information, manufacturing processes, specifications and supporting analytical information. This documents the manufacturer’s represented botanical source and FDA’s request for the underlying records; it is not FDA approval or independent confirmation of a particular proprietary conversion route. [E77 · PDF p. 12, Identity Concerns item 2]

C. Analytical and market evidence corroborate the manufacturing inference

Avula and colleagues’ analysis of 35 selected commercial products identifies 7-OH, related constituents and oxidative byproducts, and interprets compositional patterns as supporting semisynthetic origins. The authors discuss conversion of mitragynine isolates or whole extracts. These findings corroborate the manufacturing inference while remaining findings about the study’s selected samples, not a census of all national production. [E48 · PDF pp. 5–9]

Hill and colleagues catalogued 304 marketed products and inferred manufacture from purified mitragynine from the quantities marketed and the difficulty of obtaining comparable quantities of naturally occurring 7-OH. UNODC subsequently attributed its manufacturing statement to that publication. The latter is corroborating institutional recognition, not an independent second survey or factory inspection. [E37 · PDF pp. 6–7] [E22 · PDF p. 1]

Requested finding: the combined preparative, first-party commercial, regulatory-correspondence and analytical record supports designating mitragynine as the principal compound used in the documented manufacture of 7-OH, including manufacture yielding the above-threshold controlled target. If DEA requires additional evidence concerning the breadth of commercial use, the identified manufacturing and agency records in Section XI provide specific avenues for completing that finding.

VI. Prong B: an immediate chemical intermediary

The principal preparations identify mitragynine as the molecule directly converted into ordinary 7-OH. Their relevance is not limited to the fact that both molecules occur in discussions of kratom. They disclose an input-to-product chemical transformation and characterize the resulting target. [E04] [E16] [E31]

Extraction of mitragynine from a botanical matrix precedes the conversion; product work-up, separation and purification may follow it. Those physical operations do not, by themselves, establish a different intervening chemical precursor. The petition relies on the direct relationship documented in these preparations without claiming that every possible synthesis uses the same route or contains no transient reaction intermediates. DEA should make the immediacy finding on the identified transformation and exact chemical target.

The conversion-method grants expressly name mitragynine extract as the input and 7-OH as the product. The Mitradyne application separately claims enzymatic conversion of mitragynine at the C7 position to 7-OH. These claims support disclosed or likely manufacturing use, while the worked preparations supply stronger evidence that the chemical relationship has actually been demonstrated. [E08 · claim 1] [E09 · claim 1] [E10 · claim 1] [E12 · claims 7 and 19]

Samee et al.’s experimental work on processing kratom leaf preparations provides additional evidence concerning conversion in botanical material. Its reported measurement basis is dried-leaf weight; the petition does not equate that automatically with the finished-material denominator in DEA’s announced threshold. Harris et al.’s biocatalytic work on N-oxidation and substituted analogs is retained as broader chemistry context, not substituted for proof of ordinary 7-OH formation. [E38] [E41]

Requested finding: mitragynine is an immediate chemical intermediary used or likely to be used in manufacturing the controlled 7-OH target. This finding rests on preparative manufacture and its commercial relevance. The metabolic studies in the archive provide scientific context; no assertion that a consumer’s body is an illicit manufacturing facility is needed.

VII. Prong C: why precursor control is necessary

A. Control of the output leaves a distinct input problem

The effective target order will regulate the 7-OH material within its scope. It will not, merely by doing so, designate mitragynine as a controlled precursor. The documented input can therefore remain available without the direct CSA obligations that a mitragynine listing would impose, even though the technical means to convert it and a commercial market for the output already exist. The relevant risk is continued uncontrolled acquisition of that identified input for unauthorized manufacture after the target order takes effect. [L9] [E04] [E18] [E23]

The necessity argument is prospective but concrete: demonstrated conversion, actual commercial production, available source forms and concentrated output together identify a production route that direct input controls would restrict. The statutory terms “used or likely to be used” and “prevent, curtail, or limit” permit a preventive assessment. They do not require DEA to await an established post-scheduling illicit market, but they also do not excuse proof of a real manufacturing relationship. [L1]

B. Concentrated output connects this record to the anticipated threshold

The figures are the laboratories’ reported results, not independent re-testing. Their narrower contribution is evidence of source-material concentration and highly enriched output. The independent analytical literature and manufacturing account provide the accompanying context. C. Direct input controls add identifiable safeguards

A mitragynine listing would impose applicable registration, security, inventory, recordkeeping, distribution and import/export controls on the input, subject to statutory and regulatory exceptions. Registration and records would identify authorized handlers and transactions; security and inventory requirements would address diversion; controls on distribution would restrict acquisition through unrestricted channels. Sections 822 and 827 and the precursor precedents identify this additional intervention before conversion. [L11] [L12] [L7] [L8]

This is an incremental-control argument. Existing laws concerning attempts, conspiracies, controlled-substance manufacture, adulterated products or misbranding may already reach particular conduct. Their existence does not make a precursor designation redundant: the designation directly regulates the input itself without requiring every input transaction to be treated first as a completed downstream offense.

VIII. Scope, botanical material, alternatives and counterarguments

A. The requested coverage follows the molecule

The requested rule covers mitragynine and its salts in materials, compounds, mixtures and preparations because the evidence identifies both isolated mitragynine and mitragynine-containing source forms. Kures identifies recovery of mitragynine from leaf; Shaman Pharma expressly describes leaf and extracts as starting materials; the commercial account describes kratom leaf extract. A purity-based exclusion without an adequate technical basis could preserve access to the same molecule through a different matrix. [E16 · ¶ 301] [E17 · ¶¶ 9–16; PDF pp. 3–4] [E18 · PDF p. 7]

This scope would reach ordinary mitragynine-containing botanical products, including products used by consumers who do not manufacture 7-OH. That consequence is part of the requested relief. The argument for it is practical access to the identified manufacturing molecule, not an assertion that every consumer product is already scheduled or that every consumer intends conversion.

Section 811(e)’s prohibition on scheduling other substances solely because they are precursors of a designated immediate precursor must be respected. The requested rule does not separately schedule upstream reagents, biosynthetic starting compounds or a botanical species solely because it precedes mitragynine. It covers material containing the designated mitragynine molecule. DEA should expressly explain and support that distinction when adopting any mixture-inclusive rule. [L2]

B. The 7-OH exception does not decide the precursor question

The strongest scope objection is that DEA deliberately leaves some 7-OH-containing preparations outside the target listing, while the proposed mitragynine rule would reach many of those preparations. This is a consequence to justify openly. An exception from a particular target listing does not, by its terms alone, decide whether another constituent meets the immediate-precursor definition. Nevertheless, DEA should explain why the additional control is necessary in light of the target order’s policy, affected uses, evidence of recoverability and alternative measures. The requested rule cannot rest on simply relabeling the target exception as an error. [L1] [L2] [L9]

Threshold compliance must be assessed at the legally relevant stage under the issued order. Production of a covered intermediate or bulk 7-OH material is not automatically immunized by later formulation into a below-threshold retail product. Conversely, no violation is assumed merely because a process can produce covered material. This petition requests control of mitragynine through its own findings rather than a retrospective allegation against any named manufacturer.

C. Consumer and research uses deserve a reasoned response

Botanical use and research impose materially different consequences from the limited industrial uses discussed in the fentanyl-precursor rules. DEA should evaluate effects on consumers, researchers, analytical laboratories and legitimate manufacturers, including transition needs and available lawful research pathways. Evidence of consumer benefit, lower abuse liability under tested conditions or pharmacological differences does not answer whether mitragynine is a manufacturing precursor; it does bear on the agency’s assessment of necessity and scope. [E19] [E27] [E28] [E30] [E44] [L8]

The primary request is full molecule-inclusive control, with reasoned consideration of any legally available, evidence-supported exception. I do not propose a numerical precursor cutoff without evidence that it would be enforceable and would sufficiently restrict the documented supply route. If DEA concludes that narrower coverage or a higher-numbered schedule is warranted, I request a partial grant identifying its technical basis and the findings supporting the retained scope.

D. Other routes do not disappear from the analysis

Published total synthesis and biosynthesis can supply mitragynine without imported leaf. Where those routes still produce mitragynine before making 7-OH, they reinforce a molecule-based, source-neutral approach. A genuinely mitragynine-bypassing 7-OH route would be a different issue, requiring assessment of practical availability, scale and substitution. The petition does not claim mitragynine is indispensable to every conceivable route. [E31] [E33] [E29]

Angyal et al.’s total synthesis concerns MP and related pseudoindoxyls. It is retained as alternative-route evidence but does not by itself establish a commercially substitutable bypass for the particular 7-OH target relied upon here. The immediate-precursor request does not depend on the MP route. [E53]

E. Analytical identity and evidence quality are essential

Sheehan et al. demonstrate why related kratom compounds require chromatographic and mass-spectral discrimination. Their study is a reason to require appropriate identity confirmation, reference standards and validated quantitation; it is not a blanket basis for accepting or rejecting every COA. Threshold application must use the final order’s units, matrix and aggregation rules. [E32]

Complaints are allegations; affidavits are attributed sworn statements; patents disclose or claim processes; research studies support conclusions within their designs. Related patents and duplicated reports are counted once for a shared underlying proposition. Corporate identity is not inferred from the word “Shaman”: Sanders identifies specified entities, while the patent applicant is Shaman Pharma LLC. The FDA letter and commercial account are relied upon for what each actually states. [E17] [E21] [E23] [E77]

X. Proposed agency findings and regulatory text

A. Findings requested

  1. Controlled target. DEA identifies the effective Schedule I order and exact covered scope for above-threshold 7-OH. The following findings concern manufacture yielding material within that scope.
  2. Principal compound. On the preparative and corroborating commercial record, mitragynine is the principal compound used in the documented manufacture of the controlled 7-OH target. This finding rests on the first alternative in § 802(23)(A), not a finding that most mitragynine is produced for that use.
  3. Immediacy and use. Mitragynine is an immediate chemical intermediary used or likely to be used in that manufacture, as established by the direct preparative relationship and its commercial relevance.
  4. Necessity. Control is necessary to prevent, curtail or limit unauthorized manufacture through the documented route by restricting uncontrolled acquisition and diversion of the identified input before conversion.
  5. Scope. Coverage of mitragynine-containing materials and salts is justified by the disclosed and described source forms, subject to an express assessment of botanical products, research, analytical enforceability, regulatory alternatives and any supported exceptions.
  6. Schedule. Schedule I placement is authorized by § 811(e) while the target is in Schedule I and is selected on the stated precursor-control rationale. Any different permissible placement requires its own explanation.
