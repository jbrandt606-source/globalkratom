Prong 3.
The necessity of control.

The case for acting before conversion: restrict the identified manufacturing input, address alternative routes, and state the requested scope openly.

Finding requested · § 802(23)(C)

Control of mitragynine is necessary to prevent, curtail or limit manufacture of controlled above-threshold 7-OH through the documented mitragynine-based routes because it would bring the input supply under controls that apply before the finished target substance exists. This is the petition’s regulatory inference, supported and qualified below. [L1]

Prevention is an authorized purpose—not a consolation prize.

The petition does not ask DEA to wait for mass illicit production, a national overdose count, or a completed enforcement crisis. “Prevent” and “likely to be used” are part of the statute. But prevention still needs a concrete factual basis. Here, that basis is published preparative chemistry combined with commercial processing accounts and a market in the resulting substances. [L1] [E04 · Example 19] [E18 · PDF p. 7]

The necessity argument, step by step.

Established premise or proposed inferenceSupporting record and significance
1. An identified input can be converted into the target. Evidence.Worked chemistry identifies mitragynine as a preparative input to 7-OH; the requested target is effectively controlled above-threshold 7-OH. MP material is background only. [E04 · Example 19] [E17 · ¶ 75]
2. This is not only a theoretical laboratory interest. Evidence.The company account describes industrial processing of kratom extract. Sanders describes commercial sales and substantial manufacturing operations. [E18 · PDF pp. 6–7] [E23 · ¶¶ 12–17]
3. Finished-product control does not itself designate mitragynine. Legal distinction.The October 14 notice targets specified 7-OH/MP preparations; the proposed mitragynine designation would be a separate action. [L9]
4. Input control changes the lawful supply conditions before conversion. Legal mechanism.A scheduled precursor is subject to applicable registration, recordkeeping and other controlled-substance requirements. DEA used security and diversion-prevention reasoning in its norfentanyl rule. [L11] [L12] [L8 · 85 FR 21321–21322]
5. Those additional controls would restrict an identified means of manufacture. Petition inference.The requested finding is reduction of uncontrolled access and diversion, not a promise to eliminate every synthesis route. DEA should evaluate the expected effect against legitimate uses, alternatives and enforcement burdens.

What control of the input adds.

The practical difference is timing. An order addressing only a finished substance principally governs that substance and the conduct regulated in relation to it. A precursor designation additionally regulates the identified input itself. Applicable controls can reach its manufacture, distribution and storage before conversion into the target. The petition relies on that additional point of intervention. [L2] [L8]

This is not a claim that every presently unlisted precursor transaction is lawful or that existing conspiracy, attempt, drug or food laws are powerless. It is a narrower claim: a specific precursor designation would add direct controlled-substance obligations for the input. The proposed necessity finding concerns that incremental effect.

The commercial record supports urgency, not a national percentage.

The Missouri RRR complaint alleges that Shaman entities manufactured the majority of RRR’s kratom products and identifies enforcement actions and product seizures. This supports an identifiable supply relationship for investigation. It does not establish that Shaman made a majority of all national 7-OH supply, and the complaint’s allegations are not court findings. [E21 · ¶¶ 41–50; PDF pp. 8–10]

Sanders’ affidavit describes the importance of 7-OH operations to the business and the predicted effect of an injunction. Those statements support commercial scale. They do not directly quantify the effect of scheduling mitragynine. The petition makes that distinction while asking DEA to evaluate the described input supply. [E23 · ¶¶ 14 and 31; PDF pp. 4 and 8]

The manufacturing connection must still be established.

Difficulty detecting a prohibited retail product does not by itself show that controlling every mitragynine-containing material is necessary. The petition’s inference is that direct obligations on the identified feedstock would reduce uncontrolled acquisition and diversion before the target exists. That inference should be tested against actual investigations and feasible alternatives.

DEA should obtain available feedstock purchase records, conversion-site evidence, laboratory capability statements, representative quantities and assessments of substitution. It should compare scheduling with manufacturer licensing, targeted transaction reporting, stronger product enforcement and laboratory funding. Different state thresholds must be stated with their actual denominators; source-origin tests must not be confused with concentration tests.

An alternative route is a question to answer, not a fact to hide.

The accompanying petition relies only on the controlled 7-OH target. MP synthesis is retained as background and does not itself demonstrate a mitragynine-bypassing route to 7-OH.

Angyal’s reported MP total synthesis means the petition must not assert that controlling mitragynine would make all MP production impossible. The legal standard also includes curtail and limit. An alternative can reduce the practical impact of control without automatically eliminating every justification for restricting a documented route. [E53 · PDF pp. 1–6] [L1]

The archive does not establish the current commercial share, cost, or availability of that alternative. The requested agency review should address those matters rather than substitute an unsupported claim that the alternative is either commercially trivial or fully interchangeable. DEA’s norfentanyl precedent shows that route substitution matters; it does not establish what the answer must be here. [L8]

Why the request addresses the molecule in mixtures.

The primary requested rule covers mitragynine and its salts in any material, compound, mixture or preparation, including botanical preparations that contain it. The petition’s rationale is that the documented input need not be a pure isolated substance: the manufacturer’s account begins with kratom extract, and the patent record discusses mitragynine-containing source material. A pure-isolate-only rule could leave a route through mixtures available. That is the scope argument for DEA to decide. [E18 · PDF p. 7] [E17 · ¶¶ 9–16; PDF pp. 3–4]

The proposed rule would consequently reach mitragynine-containing consumer products as well as manufacturing inputs. That consequence should not be concealed or described as an automatic statutory command. DEA should assess legitimate research, consumer exposure, enforcement practicality, and any supported exception or narrower coverage. The petition proposes no arbitrary concentration threshold unsupported by an analytical record.

This is a request to control materials containing the designated molecule. It is not a request to control every upstream plant, chemical or reagent solely because it precedes another precursor. Section 811(e)’s anti-chaining limit remains applicable. [L2]

Proposed agency finding and requested investigation.

The documented preparative relationship and commercial production context support prospective control of mitragynine to restrict uncontrolled access to an identified manufacturing input. DEA should find control necessary to prevent, curtail or limit manufacture through the supported routes, after evaluating alternative production, legitimate uses and the requested mixture-inclusive scope. The petitioner requests collection of any additional supply-chain information needed for these findings; widespread illicit production is not proposed as a prerequisite.

Targeted records can strengthen this showing. Sanders identifies the original FDA response with the Answer and a separate 9,000-plus-page CID production. Those underlying records are not in this archive. The petition requests consideration of releasable or lawfully obtained manufacturing and supply information, not an assumption about what the unseen production proves. [E23 · ¶¶ 20 and 25; PDF pp. 6–7]

Priority hearing records include McCain’s Exhibit A; complete McCutchen/Pope remarks; the technical basis of the Idaho and Oklahoma estimates; and agency explanations connecting feedstock control to production deterrence. These are requests to complete the record, not assertions about unseen materials. [H08] [H06] [H07] [H04]
