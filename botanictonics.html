Arizona Sues Botanic Tonics

The State says Feel Free’s kratom risks were known while the product was marketed as wellness, energy, fitness and an alcohol alternative.

This is not another private complaint about a kratom product. The State of Arizona, through Attorney General Kris Mayes, has sued Botanic Tonics, LLC and founder Jerry Ross over the marketing and sale of feel free.

The 51-page complaint alleges that the company sold a two-ounce kratom-and-kava drink throughout Arizona while presenting it as a wellness tonic, energy product, kava drink and alternative to alcohol. Arizona alleges the marketing concealed or minimized material risks including dependence, addiction and withdrawal.

At a Glance

  • Who sued: The State of Arizona, through Attorney General Kris Mayes.
  • Who was sued: Botanic Tonics, LLC and founder Jerry Ross.
  • What Arizona alleges: Feel Free was marketed as wellness, energy, kava and an alcohol alternative while material kratom-related risks were concealed or minimized.
  • What the complaint says the company knew: Arizona alleges Ross personally experienced effects of heavy use before launch, including feeling “toxic” and the need to taper, and later acknowledged that some consumers would abuse the product.
  • Who Arizona says was targeted: The complaint describes marketing to people seeking sobriety, “sober curious” consumers, college students, athletes and ordinary convenience-store shoppers.
  • Why lawmakers should read it: The case puts product disclosure, retail placement, repeat-purchase incentives, consumer monitoring and enforcement questions into a state consumer-protection court record.
  • Status: These are allegations in a civil complaint filed September 23, 2026. They have not been adjudicated.

The State’s central allegation

Arizona’s theory is not simply that Botanic Tonics should have known more about its product. The complaint alleges that the company and its founder had direct knowledge of heavy use, dependence, withdrawal and adverse consumer experiences while continuing to expand the product’s retail footprint and marketing.

The State says Ross deliberately consumed approximately six bottles per day for several months before launch, described the amount as “abusive levels,” said he eventually “felt toxic,” and discussed the need to taper after heavy use.

From Arizona’s complaint“I knew that some people would abuse it.”
The State alleges Ross made this acknowledgment after personally testing the product at what he described as “abusive levels.”

The complaint also quotes him describing a dose-response relationship: “the more you take, the more euphoric it is.” Those statements matter because Arizona is using the defendants’ alleged knowledge and representations as part of its consumer-fraud case.

From specialty product to checkout-counter impulse buy

Arizona alleges Botanic Tonics helped transform kratom into a mainstream, ready-to-drink retail product. The complaint says feel free is sold at more than 30,000 retail locations nationwide, including hundreds in Arizona.

The State quotes Ross discussing the value of the “impulse section” near a register and citing 5-hour Energy as a model for how a small bottle can become an unplanned purchase and then a repeat purchase.

The issue Arizona puts before the court is not merely what was in the bottle. It is how the bottle was presented, where it was placed, who it was marketed to, and what the company allegedly knew.

Arizona alleges Botanic Tonics repeatedly emphasized kava and “ancient plants” while inadequately communicating that feel free also contained kratom and carried materially different risks. The complaint says the company’s website contained scores of promotional headlines focused on kava without mentioning kratom.

The State further alleges that a health-and-wellness “halo” was built around the product through natural, plant-based, fitness and athletic messaging.

Marketing to people trying not to drink

One of the complaint’s most consequential sections concerns sobriety. Arizona alleges that Botanic Tonics promoted feel free as an alcohol replacement and targeted consumers seeking to reduce or stop drinking without adequately disclosing the product’s own dependence and withdrawal risks.

The complaint alleges that the company used influencers, social-media advertising and podcasts to promote the product as an alcohol alternative. It says more than 1,000 Instagram ads targeted consumers suffering from alcohol addiction, and quotes Ross as saying people experiencing alcohol issues were “one of the main customer bases” for feel free.

Arizona also describes campaigns aimed at the “sober curious” market and younger consumers.

From Arizona’s complaintThe State alleges more than 1,000 Instagram ads targeted consumers suffering from alcohol addiction while presenting feel free as an alcohol alternative, and alleges people with alcohol problems were described as “one of the main customer bases.”

The college strategy

The complaint alleges that Botanic Tonics borrowed from the “Red Bull playbook” for what it called “college activations.” According to the State, the campaigns involved thousands of campus ambassadors, product distribution in libraries and at Greek parties, and messaging presenting feel free as an answer to student stress.

Arizona specifically alleges that an Arizona State University student worked as a distributor responsible for promoting the product to retailers, securing shelf space and obtaining store promotion.

The customers were talking. Arizona says the company was listening.

The complaint alleges that Botanic Tonics monitored consumer reports describing escalating consumption, dependence, withdrawal, financial hardship, rehabilitation and medical detoxification. It specifically discusses the Reddit community r/Quittingfeelfree and Better Business Bureau complaints.

One detail stands out: Arizona says Botanic Tonics responded to a consumer who reported consuming four to seven bottles per day by referencing that customer’s online ordering history and stating that the person appeared to be consuming more than recommended.

That allegation matters because the State says the company had visibility into unusually heavy purchasing while also operating systems designed to encourage repeat sales.

Enforcement question for lawmakersArizona alleges the company could review a customer’s ordering history, recognized consumption above its recommendations, and still operated rewards and recurring-purchase systems designed to encourage additional sales.

Rewards, subscriptions and repeat purchasing

Arizona alleges that Botanic Tonics’ Cobalt Club rewarded greater spending, with higher benefits and point multipliers as customers spent more. According to the complaint, its highest rewards level became available after $1,500 in spending.

The State also challenges the company’s subscription model. The complaint says the website defaulted consumers toward a recurring “subscribe and save” option and cites a substantial price difference between subscription and one-time purchases.

That combination is central to Arizona’s case: the State alleges the defendants knew consumers were reporting escalating use while commercial systems continued to encourage repeat and higher-volume purchasing.

Driving and power tools

Arizona also alleges that marketing encouraged consumers to drive cars, ride motorcycles and use power tools while using feel free, without adequately warning about possible opioid-like or sedative effects. The complaint cites an Instagram road-trip advertisement telling consumers to bring the product along and “Sip it anytime of day.”

For employers and policymakers concerned with safety-sensitive work, this portion of the complaint deserves particular attention.

Why this filing matters beyond Arizona

Kratom policy debates often turn on a simple assumption: that regulation can rely on accurate labels, responsible marketing, serving limits and compliant sellers.

Arizona’s complaint puts that assumption under scrutiny. The State alleges obscured kratom content, inadequate risk disclosure, wellness and energy positioning, sobriety marketing, college promotion and sales practices that encouraged repeated purchasing.

A rule on paper is only as effective as the information on the label, the product actually in the bottle, the marketing surrounding it and the ability to enforce the rule in ordinary retail commerce.

The allegations have not yet been proven. But lawmakers considering how kratom products should be regulated now have a detailed state consumer-protection complaint describing how one of the country’s most visible kratom products was allegedly marketed and sold.

What Arizona is asking the court to do

The State seeks a permanent injunction against deceptive or unfair practices, restitution for consumers, disgorgement of profits or other benefits allegedly obtained through unlawful conduct, costs and attorneys’ fees, and civil penalties of up to $10,000 for each willful violation by each defendant.

The complaint is dated September 23, 2026. The defendants will have an opportunity to answer Arizona’s allegations and present their defenses.

This filing deserves attention because it moves a long-running debate over kratom marketing out of promotional materials and into a court record.
