The "24 Million Kratom Users" Mirage

One industry-supplied survey. One enormous extrapolation. A lot of questions.

24 Million Americans

Kratom advocates have a shiny new number:

24 MILLION AMERICANS

Supposedly, that's how many Americans use kratom.

Sounds authoritative.

Until you read the study.

Nobody counted 24 million people. The number comes from extrapolating a 9.1% prevalence estimate from a 2025 survey by Oliver Grundmann and colleagues.

Read the study: 📄 Prevalence and Use Patterns of Kratom.pdf (10 pages)

And the methodology deserves considerably more attention than the talking point.

First Problem: The Study Says "Non-Probabilistic"

The title advertises a:

"US Nationally Representative Sample."

The Methods section says:

"This survey utilized non-probabilistic based sampling."

Participants came from people who had previously opted into commercial research panels maintained by Research America and its partners.

That's not a minor footnote.

When you're going to extrapolate a survey result into tens of millions of Americans, how the sample was obtained matters enormously.

Then Comes the 9.1%

The paper says 66,121 panelists were invited and 11,545 began the survey.

Then things get interesting.

Of those 11,545:

  • 10,496 either dropped out, didn't qualify, or didn't complete it.

Why might someone not qualify?

The authors tell us: "Those who did not qualify were non-kratom users."

That left exactly 1,049 completed survey responses - the kratom users.

Then the paper divides those 1,049 users by the 11,545 people who began the survey and announces:

9.1%

prevalence among the general U.S. adult population

The arithmetic isn't the interesting part.

The leap from an opt-in, non-probability online panel to the general U.S. adult population is.

Especially When Federal Data Said 0.7%

Here's the part worth putting in giant type.

The paper itself acknowledges that analysis of the federal National Survey on Drug Use and Health estimated past-year kratom use at:

0.7%
NSDUH past-year
9.1%
New study

That's roughly a 13-fold difference.

The authors also cite another nationally representative survey finding 6.1% lifetime prevalence.

A new estimate can absolutely differ from an older one.

But when it jumps from 0.7% past-year use to 9.1%, policymakers should be asking why - not simply multiplying 9.1% by America's population and repeating the result.

Surprise: Guess Who Provided the Data?

Buried near the end:

"The authors would like to thank the Global Kratom Coalition for providing the survey data for independent analysis and publication."

Yes.

The Global Kratom Coalition provided the survey data behind the study now being used to support an enormous estimate of America's kratom-using population.

The authors report no potential conflict of interest and no funding associated with the article.

Fine.

But when this statistic enters legislative debate, lawmakers deserve to know where the data came from.

Want to Check the Data Yourself?

Good luck.

The paper says the supporting data are held by Research America, that restrictions apply to their availability, and that access requires permission.

So let's recap.

  • Non-probability opt-in panel.
  • Industry coalition supplied the survey data.
  • Restricted underlying dataset.
  • Prevalence roughly 13 times the earlier federal past-year estimate.
  • And out pops:
24 MILLION!

But wait. What Does the 9.1% Even Measure?

This may be the best part.

The abstract calls the 1,049 respondents:

  • "current kratom" users.

The Results section calls them:

  • "active kratom users."

Then the Discussion calls the same 9.1%:

  • "lifetime prevalence."

Those aren't synonyms.

If you're going to extrapolate a survey result into tens of millions of Americans, deciding whether you're measuring current use, active use, or lifetime use seems like something worth sorting out first.

24 Million Wasn't Counted

  • No government agency identified 24 million kratom users.
  • No census counted them.
  • This is an extrapolation from a single survey using non-probability sampling.
  • The survey data came from the Global Kratom Coalition.
  • The underlying data aren't freely available.
  • The resulting prevalence dwarfs the earlier federal past-year estimate.
  • And the paper itself can't consistently describe whether its 9.1% represents current, active, or lifetime use.

So the next time somebody marches into a statehouse declaring:

"24 million Americans use kratom!"

Lawmakers should ask one question:

24 million according to whom?

Because 24 million Americans weren't counted.

They were manufactured by multiplication.

Methodological note: This critique is based on the publicly available study "Prevalence and Use Patterns of Kratom (Mitragyna speciosa Korth.) in a US Nationally Representative Sample" (Grundmann et al., 2025). The analysis focuses on the study's sampling methodology, data source, and reported prevalence estimates.
