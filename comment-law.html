Why This Matters to Law Enforcement

The U.S. Department of Health and Human Services (HHS) has opened a public comment period on the proposed federal scheduling threshold for 7-hydroxymitragynine (7-OH). The threshold selected will directly affect how this substance can be policed, prosecuted, and controlled.

Law enforcement organizations have a unique and important voice in this process. You understand the practical realities of enforcement: what officers can determine in the field, what crime laboratories can measure, and what prosecutors can prove beyond a reasonable doubt. HHS needs to hear from you before a threshold is set that may be difficult or impossible to enforce.

  • Your expertise matters: You know what makes a drug law enforceable.
  • Your voice provides practical perspective: Professional law enforcement input provides practical implementation perspectives that may assist HHS in evaluating the proposed threshold.
  • Your perspective is unique: No one else understands the practical enforcement challenges like you do.

The Enforcement Challenge

A percentage threshold sounds precise on paper. But in practice, it creates a series of enforcement obstacles that officers, crime laboratories, and prosecutors may not easily overcome.

HHS is considering whether to schedule 7-hydroxymitragynine based on a percentage concentration threshold. While this approach may seem scientifically rigorous, it introduces significant practical challenges for law enforcement at every stage of the enforcement process, from the traffic stop to the courtroom.

  • Field identification: Officers generally cannot determine whether a product exceeds a statutory concentration threshold during initial field encounters.
  • Laboratory burden: Crime labs must distinguish lawful from unlawful products based on precise measurements. Quantitative analysis may require additional analytical work and laboratory resources compared with simple identification.
  • Prosecutorial proof: Cases involving numerical thresholds can involve disputes regarding sampling, analytical methods, and measurement uncertainty.
  • Manufacturer adaptation: Manufacturers may seek to formulate products to comply with any legal threshold, creating ongoing compliance and enforcement considerations.

Why Percentage Thresholds Are Difficult to Police

Percentage-based thresholds create practical enforcement problems that are often overlooked in the policymaking process. Here are the key challenges law enforcement faces:

Field Testing Limitations

Officers rely on field tests to make probable cause determinations. Field tests indicate the presence of a substance; they do not measure concentration. A percentage threshold requires a determination that cannot be made at the roadside. This means officers cannot definitively establish whether a substance is lawful or unlawful during initial contact.

Crime Laboratory Workload

When a percentage threshold is in place, crime laboratories must not only identify the substance but also quantify its concentration with precision. This requires more sophisticated instrumentation, more rigorous validation protocols, and more time per case. With many labs already facing significant backlogs, this additional burden could delay investigations and prosecutions.

Prosecution and Measurement Uncertainty

In court, prosecutors must prove beyond a reasonable doubt that a substance exceeds the legal threshold. This introduces complex questions about measurement uncertainty, analytical tolerances, and laboratory error margins. Defense attorneys may challenge laboratory results, potentially turning every case into a battle over scientific methodology rather than the underlying conduct.

Evasion Through Formulation

Manufacturers may seek to formulate products to comply with any legal threshold, creating ongoing compliance and enforcement considerations. This creates a "cat and mouse" dynamic where enforcement is constantly chasing new formulations. A threshold that is low enough to be meaningful may be so low that it is practically indistinguishable from measurement error, creating further enforcement complications.

Questions HHS Should Hear From Law Enforcement

Your organization's comment should address the practical realities of enforcement. These are the questions HHS needs to consider, and your professional experience can help answer them.

How will officers determine probable cause with a percentage threshold?▾

Field tests cannot measure concentration. Officers would have to make arrests based on suspicion and then wait for laboratory analysis. This undermines efficient enforcement and creates uncertainty for officers in the field. HHS should consider whether a threshold that cannot be assessed in the field is practical for law enforcement.

What is the burden on state and local crime laboratories?▾

Quantitative analysis requires more time, more training, and more expensive equipment than qualitative identification. Many crime laboratories are already underfunded and overburdened. A percentage threshold may increase case processing time and potentially extend backlogs, delaying justice.

Will prosecutors be forced to litigate measurement uncertainty in every case?▾

Cases involving numerical thresholds can involve disputes regarding sampling, analytical methods, and measurement uncertainty. Prosecutors would need to establish not only that the substance is present, but also that its concentration exceeds the legal threshold within a margin of error. This shifts the focus of trials from conduct to scientific methodology.

Can manufacturers circumvent a percentage threshold?▾

Manufacturers may seek to formulate products to comply with any legal threshold, creating ongoing compliance and enforcement considerations. This means law enforcement will constantly face new products designed to evade enforcement. HHS should consider whether a threshold-based approach creates a regulatory "floor" that manufacturers can safely operate beneath.

How does a percentage threshold affect training and policy?▾

Law enforcement agencies would need to train officers on a new, complex threshold. Policies would need to address how to handle products near the threshold. This creates additional administrative and training burdens for already-stretched agencies.

Is there a threshold that would actually be enforceable in practice?▾

This is the central question. Law enforcement has the practical experience to advise HHS on what types of thresholds, if any, can be effectively enforced. Your organization's input on this question is essential to creating a law that actually works.
