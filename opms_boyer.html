Louisiana · Federal Court · PACER Document 249 · March 2, 2026

Can Kratom Be Ruled Out?

In a wrongful-death lawsuit involving OPMS Silver, the plaintiff challenges a defense toxicologist who attributes a fatal cardiac event to other possible causes.

← Back to OPMS Court Records

Harmony Moller died at age 36 in February 2023. In the ensuing Louisiana federal lawsuit, her mother challenges the testimony of defense medical toxicologist Dr. Edward Boyer.

The plaintiff says Dr. Boyer improperly ruled out kratom as a contributing cause and instead suggested an underlying heart abnormality or caffeine. The defense expert disputes kratom causation. The court documents present competing opinions, not a final judicial determination.

At a Glance

  • Case: Moller v. Martian Sales, Inc., et al., No. 2:24-cv-00228-WBV-DPC, Eastern District of Louisiana.
  • Filing: March 2, 2026, PACER Document 249 and 14 attachments.
  • Who filed: Kathleen Moller, the plaintiff.
  • What she seeks: To exclude defense expert Dr. Edward Boyer’s testimony under Rule 702 and Daubert.
  • What is disputed: Whether kratom contributed to Harmony Moller’s death and whether alternative explanations are sufficiently supported.
  • Status: The archive includes an unsigned proposed order, not proof the motion was granted.

The safety evidence legislators should examine

These are points drawn from the plaintiff’s memorandum and cited deposition excerpts. They are significant because they expose what remains uncertain about kratom safety, especially in people with medical conditions or taking other substances.

Safety question 1 · Document 249-1, pages 5–6No established maximum safe dose was identified for either mitragynine or 7-hydroxymitragynine in Dr. Boyer’s cited deposition testimony.

The plaintiff quotes Dr. Boyer acknowledging that he knew of no publication defining a maximum safe mitragynine dose and that no maximum safe dose had been defined for 7-OH. That does not establish that every dose is dangerous, but it is a substantial limitation on claims that a particular exposure is necessarily safe.

Safety question 2 · Document 249-1, pages 6–7The plaintiff cites five other defense experts who would not categorically rule out kratom as a contributing factor.

The memorandum points to testimony from Drs. Wang, Gandy, Huestis, McCurdy and Dollar. Their positions differ, but the plaintiff argues that they acknowledge possible contributions from kratom, including interactions and cardiac arrhythmia risks. A possibility is not proof of causation in this death.

Safety question 3 · Document 249-1, page 10The defense toxicologist reportedly knew “virtually nothing” about the specific OPMS Silver product.

The plaintiff cites deposition testimony that Dr. Boyer did not know the product’s alkaloid concentrations, ingredients or serving size and had not reviewed its testing documents. That is a product-specific knowledge challenge, not a finding that the product was defective.

Safety question 4 · Document 249-1, pages 7–8The plaintiff challenges an asserted threefold postmortem increase in mitragynine concentrations.

The memorandum argues that Dr. Boyer generalized from limited data and cites Dr. Huestis disputing that interpretation. This matters because postmortem redistribution can affect how measured drug concentrations are interpreted after death.

Safety question 5 · Document 249-1, pages 8–10The caffeine explanation is contested, and the filing says no quantitative caffeine concentration was available.

The plaintiff disputes Dr. Boyer’s caffeine-toxicity theory, citing the lack of a measured caffeine level and testimony from other experts. This does not establish that caffeine played no role; it shows why the alternative-cause claim is disputed.

What the medical examiner recorded

According to the plaintiff’s memorandum quoting the autopsy, the cause of death was “Abnormally high left coronary ostia takeoff exacerbated by mitragynine use.” The underlying cardiac anatomy is important: the disputed issue is whether kratom aggravated an existing vulnerability. The autopsy and toxicology exhibit is provided below so readers can examine the original records.

The plaintiff also points to potential interactions with other substances, including diphenhydramine and promethazine. Legislators should not treat this case as proof of a kratom-only death.

Why the plaintiff wants Dr. Boyer excluded

Under federal evidence rules, expert testimony must rest on adequate facts and reliable methods. The plaintiff argues Dr. Boyer did not perform a sufficiently reliable patient-specific causation analysis, overstated postmortem redistribution, advanced a speculative caffeine theory, and lacked sufficient knowledge of OPMS Silver.

Those are the plaintiff’s legal arguments. A motion to exclude is not itself a finding that an expert was wrong or biased. The defense has an opportunity to contest these allegations.

The practical policy question is straightforward: what safety assurances can a retail product make when experts disagree about safe exposure, interactions and the interpretation of fatal toxicology?
