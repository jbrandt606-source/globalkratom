FDA Has Refused Kratom 1,177 Times. So Why Is It Still Letting It In?

Two FDA datasets show an established refusal program alongside 180 kratom entry lines categorized as Human Food that were allowed to proceed or released since 2020.

The Numbers

1,177 kratom refusal records. 1,045 contain FDA charge 2120. Since 2020, a separate FDA entry dataset shows 180 kratom entry lines receiving admission-type dispositions. 24 were detained and later released.

The refusal and entry databases measure different things and do not establish a refusal rate. They do establish a marked change in recorded refusals while admission-type dispositions continued.

FDA's Refusal Basis

Of the 1,177 refusal records, 1,045 contain FDA charge 2120 — UNSFDIETSP. FDA's official charge language states that the article appears to be a dietary supplement or ingredient for which there is inadequate information to provide reasonable assurance that it does not present a significant or unreasonable risk of illness or injury.

The refusal history shows that FDA has repeatedly used an established regulatory mechanism against imported kratom. The investigative question is how that mechanism is being applied to later entries.

May Proceed is not FDA approval. The more significant records are the 24 entries detained and subsequently released. The public disposition data do not explain what changed between detention and release.

A 27-Ton Example

A 2026 entry associated with PT Sumber Warih Sejahtera was identified by FDA as KRATOM / Human Food, detained, and subsequently released. A closely corresponding maritime record shows 27,001 kg of Mitragyna speciosa arriving in Houston on March 5, 2026, container MRSU3972450, bill of lading MAEU263113795.

The public disposition does not identify the detention basis, the importer's response, or why FDA ultimately released the entry. Those underlying records are the next logical target for disclosure.

What the Bills of Lading Show

A separate review of U.S. maritime bills of lading identified 367 de-duplicated Mitragyna speciosa shipment records from April 2024 through July 2026, representing approximately 11,390 metric tons of cargo.

The cargo descriptions were not uniform. Within that dataset, 43 records representing approximately 1,200 metric tons contain some form of “not for human consumption” language. Another 20 records representing approximately 481 metric tons contain dye or color-use terminology. Other records use external-use terminology or non-food tariff classifications. These categories overlap and should not be added together.

The maritime data do not establish that every shipment carrying non-consumption language was entered with FDA as Human Food. That requires an exact shipment-level match. They do show why intended use, cargo description and FDA disposition should be examined together.

Where the records overlap

Several FDA entries can be closely aligned with maritime records by exporter, commodity and arrival date. One example is the 2026 PT Sumber Warih Sejahtera shipment described above: approximately 27 metric tons of Mitragyna speciosa in the maritime data and a closely corresponding FDA KRATOM / Human Food entry that was detained and later released.

Another exporter, PT Delta Gambung Nusantara, appears repeatedly in the maritime dataset. Eleven of its records use variations of “Natural Dye Mitragyna Speciosa” or “Natural Dye Mitragyna Speciosa Powder.” A February 2026 shipment from the same exporter is described on the bill of lading simply as Mitragyna speciosa; a closely corresponding FDA entry identifies the product as KRATOM / Human Food and records a May Proceed disposition. The February bill of lading itself does not say “natural dye” or “not for human consumption,” so the records should not be presented as contradictory declarations for that particular container.

Check It Yourself

  1. Open the FDA Data Dashboard. Use the public import/refusal tools.
  2. Search refusal records for kratom. Review Refused Date, Firm, Product, Shipment ID and Refusal Charges. Pay particular attention to 2120 — UNSFDIETSP.
  3. Search FDA entry data for kratom product codes in the 54J and 54K categories. Review Product Category, Manufacturer, Arrival Date and Final Disposition.
  4. Separate May Proceed from Release. Do not describe May Proceed as FDA approval. Identify Release After Detention and Released with Comment After Detention separately.
  5. Request the underlying detention files. Those records should show why FDA stopped an entry and what changed before release.

The Question

FDA's public data show an agency that has refused kratom more than a thousand times while also recording later kratom Human Food entries that were permitted to proceed or released.

The records behind the 24 detention-and-release decisions should explain how FDA is applying its kratom import policy in practice.
