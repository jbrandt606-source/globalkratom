What Is a Bill of Lading?

Think of it as the shipping industry's receipt.

Every international shipment receives one. It is a legal document issued by a carrier to a shipper that details the type, quantity, and destination of the goods being carried.

A bill of lading identifies:

  • Shipper — The exporter or seller of the goods.
  • Consignee — The importer or buyer of the goods.
  • Cargo — What is being shipped and how it is described.
  • Ports — Where the cargo was loaded and where it is being delivered.
  • Weight — The gross and net weight of the cargo.
  • Container — The unique identifier for the shipping container.
  • Transportation company — The carrier moving the goods.

For investigators and policymakers, these documents are a window into the international supply chain. They do not prove regulatory compliance or violation, but they do reveal who is trading what, with whom, and at what scale.

Why Lawmakers Should Care

Bills of lading help answer questions like:

Who imported the shipment?

The consignee field identifies the U.S. importer of record. This can reveal which companies are bringing kratom into the country.

Which foreign company exported it?

The shipper field identifies the exporter. This can reveal supply relationships and recurring trading partners.

How large was it?

The weight and container fields reveal the scale of each shipment. Some exceed 80 metric tons.

Which U.S. port received it?

The port of discharge shows where the shipment entered the country. This can reveal regional import patterns.

How was the cargo described?

The cargo description field can reveal how the product was categorized, including phrases like "Not For Human Consumption."

What bills cannot tell us

Bills of lading do not establish FDA review, customs admissibility, or whether a law was violated. They are logistical documents, not enforcement records.

Bills of lading are a starting point for investigation, not a conclusion. They identify patterns worthy of further inquiry, but they do not prove how any particular shipment was handled by regulators.

Master Bill vs House Bill

One shipment may appear multiple times in public databases. Understanding the difference between a master bill of lading and a house bill of lading explains why.

Master Bill of Lading
⬇
House Bill A House Bill B House Bill C
One cargo ship · Multiple consignees · Multiple documents

A master bill is issued by the carrier to the freight forwarder. A house bill is issued by the freight forwarder to each individual shipper or consignee. When a container holds goods for multiple buyers, the master bill covers the entire container while each house bill covers a portion of the cargo.

This is why your workbook reduced 500 source records to 367 curated shipments. Multiple bills of lading can refer to the same physical container, and deduplication is necessary to avoid double-counting.

Investigative implication: When you see a shipment appearing multiple times in a database, it may not be multiple shipments. It may be multiple documents for the same container. Understanding master and house bills helps avoid counting the same cargo twice.

Wording varies. Some records describe the product with scientific precision. Others use generic or vague terms. Some explicitly state "Not For Human Consumption" or "External Use."

Public shipping records alone do not explain why these different descriptions appear. They may originate with the shipper, the freight forwarder, the carrier, or the importer. They may reflect how the product was marketed, how it was classified, or how it was documented for shipping purposes.

Important: The cargo description on a bill of lading is not necessarily the same as the customs entry description. It is a shipping document, not a regulatory filing. The relationship between the bill-of-lading description and the customs declaration is not established by public shipping records.

Reading the HTS Code

Every internationally traded product receives a customs classification under the Harmonized Tariff Schedule (HTS). The tariff classification helps U.S. Customs and Border Protection determine duty treatment and collect trade statistics.

The records document the classifications that were used. They do not establish why different classifications appear. A different tariff heading may affect customs classification, duty treatment, and how the merchandise is described in trade data. But the public records do not establish whether the classification altered FDA screening or admissibility review.

Note: 1211.90 and 3101.00 are six-digit international HS-level headings. A complete U.S. HTS classification ordinarily extends beyond six digits. The public records analyzed for this investigation did not always contain the full ten-digit HTS code.

Container Numbers

Every ocean container has a unique identifier.

TLKU 4582712

This four-letter prefix (often the carrier code) followed by a six-digit serial number and a check digit is unique to that container.

Container numbers let investigators connect documents referring to the same physical container. When a master bill and a house bill share the same container number, it is evidence that they refer to the same cargo movement.

In the records analyzed, container numbers were essential for deduplicating shipments and ensuring that the same cargo was not counted multiple times.

Why Some Bills Look Different

Not all bills of lading are the same. Several variations appear in public shipping records.

Regular Bill of Lading

  • Issued by the carrier to the shipper
  • Standard format with all key fields
  • Used for most commercial shipments

Master Bill of Lading

  • Issued by the carrier to the freight forwarder
  • Covers an entire container or vessel
  • May not list individual consignees

House Bill of Lading

  • Issued by the freight forwarder to the shipper
  • Covers a portion of a master bill
  • Lists the actual consignee

Sea Waybill

  • Non-negotiable document
  • Does not function as a document of title
  • More common for less formal arrangements

Different freight forwarders and different carriers may use different formats. Some documents are more detailed than others. Some include additional fields like "Notify Party" or "Place of Delivery."

These variations are normal in international shipping. They do not indicate irregularity. But they do mean that investigators must carefully compare documents to ensure they are comparing like with like.

What Bills of Lading Cannot Tell Us

This is very important.

Bills of lading are logistical documents, not enforcement records. They cannot tell us:

  • Whether FDA reviewed a shipment
  • Whether it was detained or released
  • Whether Customs accepted the classification
  • What happened after delivery
  • Whether the imported material entered a retail product
  • Whether any law was violated

Bills of lading are not evidence of regulatory compliance or violation. They are transportation records. They document what moved, not whether it was legal.

To determine FDA review or customs admissibility, one needs access to customs entry documents and FDA records.

The purpose of analyzing bills of lading is not to prove wrongdoing. It is to identify patterns, ask informed questions, and direct further investigation.
