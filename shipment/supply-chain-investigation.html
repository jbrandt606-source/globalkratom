Opening

Imagine walking into a gas station.

You see a bottle. A pouch. A capsule.

What you do not see is the international supply chain that delivered it.

Before reaching an American shelf, that product may have crossed an ocean, passed through multiple companies, been documented on customs forms, assigned an international tariff classification, and transported through warehouses thousands of miles away.

This investigation follows that paper trail.

Using publicly available shipping records, trade databases, and customs documentation, we have reconstructed the movement of kratom from Southeast Asian farms to American warehouses. The records do not prove regulatory violations. They do not establish intent. But they do reveal the industrial scale, the recurring patterns, and the documentation choices that define how kratom enters the United States.

This is not an opinion piece. This is an evidence-based briefing for lawmakers, policy analysts, and anyone seeking to understand the kratom supply chain.

This investigation is based on 500 public shipping records, 367 unique shipments, and 11,390 metric tons of identified kratom imports. The data was compiled from import databases, customs filings, and bills of lading available to the public.

Source: ImportYeti, ImportGenius, Volza Trade Data, and public customs records.

The purpose of this investigation is not to determine guilt. It is to understand how kratom enters the United States.

Editor's note

How International Shipping Works

To understand the kratom supply chain, it helps to understand how international shipping works. Every product that enters the United States by ocean freight passes through a predictable series of steps.

The following diagram illustrates the typical journey of a kratom shipment from farm to retail shelf.

Farmer → Processor → Exporter → Container → Ocean Carrier → United States Port → Importer → Warehouse → Retail Product

Each step generates documentation. The bill of lading is created when the cargo is loaded onto the vessel. The customs entry is filed when the shipment arrives at the port. The importer of record is responsible for clearing the goods through customs.

Public shipping records capture some of these documents, but not all. The records that are available provide a partial view of the supply chain, but they are often sufficient to identify patterns and raise questions.

What Is a Bill of Lading?

Think of it as the shipping industry's receipt.

A bill of lading is a legal document issued by a carrier to a shipper that details the type, quantity, and destination of the goods being carried. It serves three functions:

  1. Receipt — It acknowledges that the carrier has received the cargo.
  2. Contract — It serves as the contract of carriage between the shipper and the carrier.
  3. Title — It represents ownership of the goods and can be used to transfer title.

In the context of kratom imports, the bill of lading contains critical information:

  • Shipper — The exporter or seller of the goods.
  • Consignee — The importer or buyer of the goods.
  • Port of Loading — Where the container was loaded onto the vessel.
  • Port of Discharge — Where the container arrived in the United States.
  • Weight — The gross and net weight of the cargo.
  • Cargo Description — What is being shipped.
  • Container Number — The unique identifier for the shipping container.

The cargo description is where the most interesting details appear. Some bills of lading describe the product as "Mitragyna speciosa" while others use more generic terms. Some explicitly state "Not For Human Consumption" while others do not.

These differences are not accidental. They represent deliberate choices made by the exporter, the importer, or both.

What Is an HTS Code?

Every product that enters the United States must be classified under the Harmonized Tariff Schedule (HTS).

Coffee gets one. Cars get one. Bananas get one. Kratom gets one.

The HTS is a numerical system used by U.S. Customs and Border Protection to identify goods for trade and tariff purposes. The code determines the duty rate, the statistical category, and the regulatory requirements for the product.

In the records analyzed for this investigation, the overwhelming majority of kratom shipments were classified under:

HTS Code
1211.90
Other plants and parts used in perfumery, pharmacy, or for insecticidal, fungicidal or similar purposes, fresh or dried.

Some shipments were also classified under HTS 3101, which covers animal or vegetable fertilizers. This classification has appeared in multiple records and raises questions about how kratom is being categorized for import purposes.

The choice of HTS code matters. It affects what duties are paid, what inspections are performed, and what regulatory requirements apply. If a product is classified as a fertilizer, it may not be subject to the same scrutiny as a dietary ingredient.

What Is FDA Import Alert 54-15?

FDA Import Alert 54-15 is a Detention Without Physical Examination (DWPE) alert covering dietary supplements and bulk dietary ingredients containing kratom.

The alert instructs FDA field staff to detain shipments of kratom-containing products without physically examining them, based on the agency's determination that these products may be adulterated or misbranded.

What this means in practice is that any shipment of kratom that appears to be intended for human consumption as a dietary supplement may be detained at the port of entry. The importer must then provide evidence that the product complies with FDA regulations.

However, the alert does not apply to all kratom shipments. It specifically covers dietary supplements and bulk dietary ingredients. Products that are not marketed as dietary supplements may not be subject to the alert.

This creates a distinction that is critical to understanding the supply chain. If a shipment is documented as "Not For Human Consumption," it may fall outside the scope of Import Alert 54-15. The FDA may not have the same authority to detain it.

What shipping records cannot tell us: Whether any specific shipment was reviewed by FDA, whether it was referred for examination, whether it was detained or released, or what intended-use declaration was provided to Customs.

These questions require access to customs and FDA records that are not publicly available.

The Scale of the Industry

One shipment can weigh 80 metric tons.

The analysis identified 367 unique shipments representing 11,390 metric tons.

To put that in perspective:

  • 11,390 metric tons is approximately 25 million pounds.
  • That is enough raw material to fill more than 1,000 shipping containers.
  • At a typical retail dose of 3 grams, this volume could supply billions of individual servings.

This is not a cottage industry. This is industrial-scale agricultural trade.

The records show that kratom enters the United States in large quantities, through multiple ports, from multiple suppliers, and is received by multiple importers. The volume is substantial enough that it cannot be attributed to a few isolated shipments.

What the Records Reveal

The public shipping records analyzed for this investigation reveal several patterns worth noting.

Different Descriptions

Some records say "Mitragyna speciosa." Others say "kratom." Some say "herbal powder." The descriptions are not consistent.

Different HTS Codes

Most shipments use HTS 1211.90. Some use HTS 3101 (fertilizer). The choice of code varies by shipment.

Different Importers

Multiple U.S. companies appear as importers of record. Some import large volumes, others import small quantities.

Different Suppliers

Exporters are based in Indonesia, primarily in West Kalimantan. Some suppliers appear repeatedly, others only once.

Recurring Exporters

Several Indonesian companies appear in multiple shipments. They appear to be commercial exporters with established relationships.

Industrial Scale

The volume of shipments, the size of containers, and the frequency of imports indicate a mature supply chain.

These patterns do not prove wrongdoing. But they do establish that kratom is being imported at industrial scale through a variety of channels and with varying documentation choices.

What the Records Do Not Reveal

This is very important.

The public shipping records analyzed for this investigation do not establish:

  • FDA review — Whether the FDA reviewed any specific shipment.
  • Customs decisions — Whether Customs referred any shipment to FDA or cleared it for entry.
  • Final retail destination — Where the imported material ultimately ended up.
  • Regulatory violations — Whether any shipment violated U.S. law.
  • Consumer exposure — Whether any consumer was exposed to kratom from any specific shipment.

The records are a starting point, not a conclusion. They identify patterns worthy of investigation, but they do not prove anything about how any particular shipment was handled by regulators.

Shipping records are not enforcement records. They are logistical documents.

To determine how FDA evaluated any specific shipment, one would need access to FDA records, customs entry documents, and potentially court records. Those documents are not publicly available in most cases.

Questions Raised

The records analyzed for this investigation raise several questions that policymakers and regulators may wish to explore.

Was FDA involved?

Were any of these shipments reviewed by the Food and Drug Administration? If so, what was the outcome?

Were shipments reviewed?

How many shipments were actually examined by FDA? How many were detained?

Why different descriptions?

Why do some bills of lading describe the product as "Mitragyna speciosa" while others use generic or vague terms?

Why "external use"?

Why do some shipments state "External Use" or "Not For Human Consumption" on the bill of lading?

Why different tariff codes?

Why are some kratom shipments classified as fertilizer (HTS 3101) while others are classified as botanical material (HTS 1211.90)?

How old is imported kratom?

How long does imported kratom remain in storage before it reaches consumers? What is the age of the material in retail products?

These questions are not accusations. They are legitimate inquiries based on patterns observed in public records. The answers would provide valuable context for understanding the kratom supply chain.
