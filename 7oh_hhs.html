DEA Revises 7-OH Scheduling. The Kratom Leaf Exception Remains.

October 9, 2026 · New federal notices will target enhanced 7-OH and mitragynine pseudoindoxyl using concentration and alkaloid-ratio thresholds. The source material raises a separate policy question.

Federal policy update · October 9, 2026

Washington changed the thresholds. It did not schedule kratom leaf.

The Department of Justice announced that the Drug Enforcement Administration will issue two new Notices of Intent for four kratom-related opioid substances. The most consequential change concerns 7-hydroxymitragynine (7-OH) and mitragynine pseudoindoxyl (MGPI, also called MP): a revised HHS recommendation uses product concentration and the ratio to mitragynine to distinguish enhanced products from botanical kratom.

This is a scheduling announcement, not a declaration that 7-OH has already entered Schedule I. DEA has not yet issued a temporary scheduling order for 7-OH. Meanwhile, existing temporary Schedule I controls on MGPI, MGM-15 and MGM-16 remain in effect during the renewed notice period.

The federal government is drawing a sharper line around the finished product. The question of what happens to the legal botanical source remains.

Two tests for 7-OH and pseudoindoxyl

HHS recommends Schedule I control when either of two criteria is met for 7-OH, MGPI, or their combined amount where both are present:

  • Concentration: more than 1 milligram per gram in a solid product, or more than 1 milligram per milliliter in a liquid product.
  • Ratio: a mitragynine-to-7-OH, mitragynine-to-MGPI, or mitragynine-to-combined-7-OH-and-MGPI ratio of less than 100:1.

These are alternative criteria, not two requirements that must both be satisfied. The ratio criterion is especially significant: a product could have a comparatively small absolute amount of one substance yet still meet the proposed ratio test.

How these measures will operate in practice depends on the actual Federal Register notices, definitions, validated laboratory methods and subsequent DEA orders. The announcement alone should not be mistaken for final regulatory text.

HHS now acknowledges possible trace pseudoindoxyl in botanical material

The October 9 DOJ announcement says the updated HHS analysis identifies scientific information suggesting that MGPI may occur at trace levels in some botanical kratom or kratom-derived products. HHS also says the science is still developing. That is a meaningful change from the July 2026 HHS announcement, which described MP as not naturally occurring in the plant.

The revised recommendation is intended to distinguish enhanced products from products whose alkaloid composition is more consistent with botanical kratom. Evidence of trace natural occurrence is not evidence that every retail product contains only naturally occurring quantities. Nor does it establish that every detectable MGPI concentration was produced naturally rather than through processing or conversion.

What happens to the upstream source?

Natural kratom leaf supplies mitragynine, the major alkaloid from which related opioid-active compounds can be chemically derived. Drawing a legal line between botanical material and concentrated downstream products may address specific finished formulations while leaving the supply of upstream mitragynine untouched.

This is a distinct issue from whether HHS selected scientifically appropriate thresholds. Scheduling enhanced 7-OH does not, by itself, schedule mitragynine or Mitragyna speciosa. Manufacturers' ability to use a legally available upstream alkaloid to produce other substances remains a separate question for lawmakers.

A threshold can define the product the government intends to control. It does not, by itself, close the supply chain that makes the product possible.

What this announcement does not establish

It does not establish that 7-OH has already been temporarily scheduled; that MGPI, MGM-15 or MGM-16 have been removed from Schedule I; that a product below both proposed thresholds is necessarily safe or lawful for sale; or that the revised notices settle every question about natural occurrence and commercial conversion.

It also does not make kratom leaf an FDA-approved medicine or establish that kratom products comply with food and dietary supplement law. Controlled-substance scheduling and FDA marketing requirements are separate legal questions.
