FDA Authority & Kratom:
Myth vs Fact

Understanding what the FDA can—and cannot—do under current federal law regarding kratom, dietary supplements, and emerging alkaloid products.

Common Myths — and the Facts
Myth

“If kratom were dangerous, FDA would just ban it.”

FDA cannot instantly ban a product simply because safety concerns exist. Dietary supplement regulation under DSHEA (1994) limits FDA authority unless specific statutory thresholds are met or Congress/DEA take additional action.

Fact

No instant ban authority.

FDA must follow a complex process that often requires proving a product is adulterated or misbranded. Even then, removal from the market can be slow and resource‑intensive.

Myth

“FDA approval is required before supplements are sold.”

Unlike prescription drugs, most dietary supplements do not require premarket approval before entering commerce. Manufacturers are generally responsible for ensuring safety and compliance — not the FDA.

Fact

No pre‑market approval for most supplements.

FDA primarily acts after products are already on the market, using enforcement tools like warning letters, import alerts, and recalls.

Myth

“If a product is sold in stores, FDA has determined it is safe.”

Retail availability does not equal FDA approval or safety determination. Many products remain on the market while investigations, enforcement actions, recalls, or litigation are ongoing.

Fact

Safety is not pre‑judged.

FDA has repeatedly warned about kratom’s risks, but legal and procedural hurdles mean harmful products can remain accessible for years.

Myth

“FDA has no concerns about kratom.”

FDA has repeatedly issued warnings regarding kratom‑related risks, including liver toxicity, seizures, substance use disorder, neonatal abstinence syndrome, contamination, and death.

Fact

Strong, documented concerns.

Since 2014, FDA has issued import alerts, warning letters, public health advisories, and even a mandatory recall order for kratom products.

Myth

“FDA can remove every kratom product immediately.”

FDA enforcement authority is constrained by statutory standards, evidentiary burdens, resource limitations, and the structure of federal dietary supplement law.

Fact

Enforcement is case‑by‑case and slow.

Proving adulteration, litigating against hundreds of vendors, and coordinating with customs and DOJ takes years — not days.

Myth

“Import alerts mean a substance is fully banned.”

Import alerts allow detention of certain products entering commerce but do not automatically prohibit all domestic distribution or possession.

Fact

Import alerts ≠ total prohibition.

Import Alert 54‑15 detains kratom dietary supplements at ports, but domestic sales of existing inventory may continue unless further action is taken.

Myth

“7‑OH and concentrated alkaloid products are regulated the same way as traditional drugs.”

Regulatory frameworks for emerging alkaloid products remain complex and evolving, especially when products are marketed as supplements despite opioid‑like pharmacology.

Fact

Regulatory gaps exist.

FDA has recently targeted 7‑OH products (e.g., warning letters to Hydroxie), but novel synthetic or concentrated alkaloids can appear faster than agency action.

What FDA Can — and Cannot — Easily Do

✓ What FDA Can Do

  • Issue warning letters to companies
  • Issue import alerts (e.g., Alert 54‑15)
  • Pursue voluntary or mandatory recalls
  • Seize adulterated products via US Marshals
  • Challenge unlawful drug or disease claims
  • Issue public health advisories
  • Refer substances for scheduling review (DEA)
  • Inspect manufacturing facilities

✗ What FDA Cannot Easily Do

  • Instantly remove every product nationwide
  • Require premarket approval for all supplements
  • Unilaterally schedule substances without DEA
  • Criminalize possession independently
  • Inspect every retailer or online seller
  • Rapidly litigate hundreds of violative products
  • Compel states to ban substances

Why Supplement Law Matters: DSHEA 1994

Dietary Supplement Health and Education Act (DSHEA) fundamentally changed how FDA regulates supplements. Under DSHEA:

  • Supplements are generally marketed without pre‑approval
  • Manufacturers – not FDA – are responsible for ensuring safety
  • FDA often acts after products enter commerce
  • Proving adulteration or misbranding can be legally complex and slow
  • New dietary ingredients (NDIs) require notification, but enforcement is limited

Kratom has never been lawfully marketed as a dietary ingredient under DSHEA, yet its widespread availability reflects enforcement gaps, not FDA approval or safety.

Federal Actions Related to Kratom (Selected)
2014
FDA issues Import Alert 54‑15, allowing detention of kratom dietary supplements without physical examination.
2016
US Marshals seize nearly 90,000 bottles of kratom supplements (RelaKzpro) worth >$400,000.
2017
FDA issues public health advisory warning consumers not to use kratom.
2018
First mandatory recall order issued for kratom products (Salmonella contamination).
2019–2023
Multiple warning letters to companies making unproven opioid‑withdrawal and disease claims.
2024
FDA warns consumers against OPMS Black Liquid Kratom after reports of death and addiction.
2025
FDA issues warning letter to Hydroxie (7‑OH products) as unapproved new drugs and unsafe food additives.

Why This Matters for Policymakers

Public‑health concern, retail availability, and federal enforcement complexity can coexist simultaneously. Understanding the limits of FDA authority helps explain why state legislatures, boards of pharmacy, and other agencies continue to debate regulatory approaches to kratom and emerging alkaloid products. Absence of a federal ban does not equal safety — it reflects the structural reality of supplement law and enforcement priorities.
