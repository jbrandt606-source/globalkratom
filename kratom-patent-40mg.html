40 mg of Mitragynine: The Policy Proposal and the Patent

Three researchers helped propose a regulatory ceiling. The same three are named inventors on a University of Florida kratom formulation patent. Why wasn't the patent disclosed?

REGULATORY ACCOUNTABILITY · NOVEMBER 8, 2026

The finding

Three authors of a proposed 40 mg kratom serving limit are also inventors on a University of Florida formulation application. The paper’s published conflict statement does not mention it.

The overlap raises a transparency question. It does not establish hidden revenue, improper influence, or what the authors privately disclosed to the journal.

Read the primary documents ↓

01 · THE 40 MG PROPOSAL

What the policy paper recommends

The October 22, 2026 Frontiers policy article recommends 40 mg mitragynine per serving, with no more than two servings daily for kratom leaf and Category 1 traditional preparations.

It also conditionally extends that recommendation to certain extracts: no more than 40 mg per serving, mitragynine above 40% of total alkaloids, and 7-hydroxymitragynine below 0.01% of final dry weight. Extract sellers should also establish chemical integrity, obtain safety data, and submit a new dietary ingredient notification.

A proposal, not an enacted rule. Section 3.3 cites existing studies but presents no new experiment deriving 40 mg as a uniquely validated threshold.
02 · THE PATENT

Three names on both documents

Christopher R. McCurdy, Abhisheak Sharma, and Erin Berthold coauthored the policy paper and are named inventors on US20260151446A1. Elizabeth Maxwell is a fourth inventor.

The US record lists University of Florida Research Foundation, Inc. as assignee; the international record lists both the university and Foundation. The technology uses solubility-enhancing ingredients to improve oral delivery of kratom extract or mitragynine, with a focus on canine pain treatment.

Timeline: Priority October 28, 2022 → international filing October 30, 2023 → international publication May 2, 2024 → US publication June 4, 2026 → policy manuscript received July 1, 2026.

A published application is not a granted patent, approved treatment, or proof of commercialization. Google Patents labels the US record “Pending” and the international record “Ceased”; these qualified labels do not resolve every national family member’s status.

  • 40 mg capsules prompted reformulation. Two of three dogs showed adverse effects, including restlessness and reduced appetite, at different weight-adjusted doses. Researchers then produced a 10 mg capsule. Capsule strength was not each dog’s total administered dose.
  • Rat data showed greater delivery. Table 5 reports about 69% oral bioavailability for the capsule, versus 25% for tea and 41% for a commercial extract. Dose-normalized systemic exposure was approximately 2.8 and 1.7 times higher, respectively, in groups of four rats.
  • Human effects remain unproven. These animal findings do not establish a human safety threshold or a fixed human exposure advantage. The patent narrative gives inconsistent exposure comparisons; the figures above use its dose-normalized table values.

Sources: US Claims 6 and 11; Examples 4–6 and Table 5. The international publication contains the same dosage distinctions.

04 · THE COMMERCIAL QUESTION

A milligram ceiling does not cap absorption

Equal labeled doses can produce different blood exposure. If enhanced-delivery technology qualified under the proposed framework, it could deliver more mitragynine per permitted serving than conventional preparations. Greater exposure could also increase adverse effects.

Eligibility is unresolved. The patent’s concentrated extract is not automatically a traditional botanical preparation. Certain extract embodiments might seek the paper’s conditional pathway; purified-mitragynine or disease-treatment products could instead fall under drug regulation.

The question: Would the framework permit enhanced-delivery extracts under the same ceiling—and what evidence would establish comparable safety? No licensing revenue, actual market advantage, or commercial motive has been established.
05 · THE DISCLOSURE QUESTION

What readers were told

Author EB was employed by Planted in Science Consulting, LLC. OG and CRM serve as expert witness in kratom-involved legal cases.

The statement then says the remaining authors declared no commercial or financial relationships that could constitute a potential conflict. It discloses Berthold’s employment and Grundmann’s and McCurdy’s expert-witness work. It does not identify the patent application or inventor royalty interests.

Frontiers’ current policy requires disclosure of actual and potential conflicts at submission. The patent family was public before the policy manuscript arrived at the journal.

Why was it absent from the published statement? The underlying declarations, editorial correspondence, royalty arrangements, and archived submission-date policy have not been obtained. A disclosure violation therefore has not been established.

06 · QUESTIONS REQUIRING ANSWERS

Who should explain what

  • McCurdy, Sharma, and Berthold: Were your patent interests disclosed to Frontiers? Were potential commercial interests considered when developing the recommendations?
  • Frontiers: Were any patent interests omitted from the published statement? Who assessed their relevance, and under which policy?
  • The inventors and UF Research Foundation: Do existing or prospective royalty rights exist? Has the technology been licensed or commercialized?
  • The policy authors: What independent evidence supports precisely 40 mg per serving and two servings daily?
  • The authors and Foundation: Would the proposed categories permit or favor any patented formulation, and what exposure-based safety evidence would be required?

These are unresolved questions, not interview requests sent during preparation of this page. No response or refusal is implied.
