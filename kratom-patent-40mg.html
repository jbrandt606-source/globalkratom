40 mg of Mitragynine: The Policy Proposal and the Patent

Three researchers helped propose a regulatory ceiling. The same three are named inventors on a University of Florida kratom formulation patent. Why wasn't the patent disclosed?

REGULATORY ACCOUNTABILITY · PRIMARY-DOCUMENT INVESTIGATION

One number. Two public records.

A proposed product standard measures mitragynine in milligrams. A University of Florida patent application describes ways to deliver more of it into the bloodstream. Three researchers appear on both documents: Christopher R. McCurdy, Abhisheak Sharma, and Erin Berthold. Their overlap creates a concrete transparency question for a policy paper intended to guide governments and the kratom industry.

The October 22, 2026 Frontiers in Pharmacology article recommends a 40 mg serving limit for kratom leaf and traditional preparations, with a conditional pathway for some extracts. The patent application includes a composition claim spanning 5–40 mg mitragynine. The article’s published conflict-of-interest statement does not mention the application.

That public omission deserves an explanation. It does not establish what the authors privately declared to the journal, whether any inventor receives royalties, or whether the proposed framework would confer a commercial advantage. The documents do establish why those questions matter.

01 · THE 40 MG PROPOSAL

A ceiling intended to shape the market

“Defining kratom: evidence-based policy guidelines for a rapidly evolving market” is a policy article by Erin Berthold, Paula N. Brown, Michael Chan, Oliver Grundmann, Christopher R. McCurdy, Abhisheak Sharma, and Darshan Singh. It proposes definitions and regulatory recommendations; it does not enact a legal limit.

Section 3.3 recommends a maximum of 40 mg mitragynine per serving and no more than two servings in 24 hours, totaling 80 mg daily. Its stated assurance applies to properly prepared and directed use of kratom leaf products and Category 1 traditional botanical preparations. The paper defines leaf material by its composition, including 0.5%–2.5% mitragynine by dried-leaf weight and total major alkaloids below 3%. Category 1 includes aqueous teas or decoctions and certain fluidized extracts with less than 10% alkaloid content.

The recommendation does not stop there. The authors acknowledge insufficient evidence for definitive recommendations on Category 2 concentrated extracts. They nevertheless suggest that the same recommendation might apply conditionally where a product has no more than 40 mg mitragynine per serving, mitragynine exceeds 40% of its total alkaloids, and 7-hydroxymitragynine is below 0.01% of final dry product weight. Sellers of Category 2 and 3a extracts are also urged to demonstrate no chemical alteration, obtain ingredient safety data, and submit a new dietary ingredient notification.

That conditional extension is central to the commercial question. The paper treats isolates inconsistent with natural alkaloid ratios (Category 3b) and synthetic or chemically altered products (Category 4) as novel drugs. A 40 mg label alone would not place every formulation within the recommended botanical framework.

For the precise ceiling, Section 3.3 cites a collection of observational, survey, and clinical literature, including Coe and colleagues’ 2026 study. It does not present a new experiment deriving 40 mg as a uniquely validated threshold. Regulators should ask for the explicit evidentiary bridge from those studies to this number, its daily limit, and its application across preparations with different absorption profiles.

Source: Policy paper, Section 3.3, “Serving size limitations”; product definitions in Section 1.1.

02 · THE PATENT

The same researchers, a different kind of standard

The US application US20260151446A1, “Improved oral bioavailability formulation for mitragyna speciosa (kratom)”, names Erin C. Berthold, Christopher Robert McCurdy, Abhisheak Sharma, and Elizabeth Maxwell as inventors. Berthold, McCurdy, and Sharma also appear on the policy paper. Maxwell does not.

The US publication lists University of Florida Research Foundation, Inc. as original and current assignee. The related international publication, WO2024092269A1, lists both the University of Florida and its Research Foundation. These are institutional assignee listings; inventor status is distinct from ownership, and the listings do not establish individual royalty contracts or a complete chain-of-title audit.

The family claims priority to an October 28, 2022 US provisional application, 63/420,216. The international application, PCT/US2023/078192, was filed October 30, 2023 and published May 2, 2024. Google Patents records the related US application, 19/123,532, with the October 30, 2023 filing date and June 4, 2026 publication date. Those dates predate the policy manuscript’s July 1, 2026 receipt and October 22 publication.

The technology combines kratom extract or mitragynine with solubility-enhancing excipients. Disclosed ingredients include a polyanionic beta-cyclodextrin derivative such as Captisol, crospovidone such as Kollidon CL, and microcrystalline cellulose such as Avicel. Preferred examples use extract described as 45% mitragynine. The aim is better dissolution and oral delivery, rather than merely increasing the amount printed on a label.

The specification focuses on pain treatment, particularly canine osteoarthritis, while also discussing possible human pharmaceutical development. It acknowledges NIH support under R01 DA047855 and UH3 DA048353 and states that the government has certain rights in the invention.

Application, not established commercial exclusivity. A published application contains requested claims; it is not proof that those claims have been granted, that regulators have approved a product, or that a product has been sold. Google Patents labels the US application “Pending” and the international record “Ceased”; its status labels are expressly qualified. An international record’s status does not by itself determine the status of every national family member.

Sources: US bibliographic record and specification; international family record and claims. Legal status and ownership descriptions here follow those records, not an independent patent-office prosecution or assignment audit.

03 · WHAT THE PATENT CLAIMS

Read the units before drawing the connection

Claims 6 and 11 in both publications describe different amounts and different compositions. Treating every occurrence of “40 mg” as 40 mg of mitragynine would misstate the record.

The policy limit and the application’s dosage claims
DocumentQuantityWhat it means
Policy, Section 3.340 mg mitragynine per serving; two servings dailyRecommended for leaf and Category 1, with a conditional extension to certain extracts.
Claim 6About 10–40 mg extract powder; or about 5–20 mg mitragynineDepends on the kratom-composition claims. The 40 mg extract figure is not 40 mg of pure mitragynine.
Claim 115–40 mg mitragynine; particularly 5–20 mg; more particularly about 10 mgDepends on Claim 8’s mitragynine-and-cyclodextrin composition. This is the explicit 5–40 mg mitragynine range.

Claim 11 therefore supplies a genuine numerical overlap with the policy ceiling. It does not claim ownership of the number 40 mg or every product containing that dose. Its scope depends on the composition requirements and, ultimately, any claims actually allowed.

The 40 mg canine capsule was reformulated

Example 5 describes a tolerability study in three volunteer dogs using capsules containing 40 mg mitragynine each. One dog received 3–4 mg/kg and showed dysphoria, whining, restlessness, house-soiling, and refusal to eat; its owner withdrew it. A second received 2 mg/kg and showed whining, restlessness, and reduced interest in food. A third received 1 mg/kg and tolerated it. Capsule strength and administered dose per kilogram are different measurements: these dogs were not all given an identical 40 mg total dose.

After reactions in two of the three dogs, the researchers diluted the formulation with Avicel to produce a 10 mg capsule, a fourfold reduction in capsule strength. Example 6 reports an unchanged in vitro dissolution profile after dilution. It also reports rat testing of the lower dose. The canine observations cannot identify a human safety threshold, and they do not demonstrate that a 40 mg human serving would cause the same effects.

What the exposure data actually support

In male rats, Table 5 reports absolute oral bioavailability of approximately 69% for the inventive capsule, compared with 25% for lyophilized kratom tea, 41% for an OPMS Gold liquid extract, and 37% for an oral mitragynine solution. The comparisons used different administered doses and small groups: the table reports four animals per group.

  • Peak concentration per unit doseThe capsule’s dose-normalized Cmax was 46.1, versus 11.1 for tea and 11.7 for the commercial extract—approximately fourfold higher.
  • Systemic exposure per unit doseDose-normalized AUC was 232.9 for the capsule, versus 83.7 for tea and 136.1 for the commercial extract—approximately 2.8-fold and 1.7-fold higher, respectively.
  • Bioavailability requires careful arithmeticThe table implies roughly 44 percentage points more than tea and 28 points more than the commercial extract. The specification’s phrasing of increases of “45 and 29%” should not be mistaken for unambiguous relative-percentage increases.

The narrative also gives differing exposure comparisons: one passage reports 18-fold and threefold increases against tea and the commercial solution, while the next summarizes approximately threefold and twofold increases. The table’s dose-normalized values provide the clearest basis for the comparisons above. Those narrative figures should not be repeated as interchangeable measures of the same effect.

Table 7 adds another limit: in male rats, reducing the dose from 8 to 2 mg/kg reduced peak concentration about fourfold but AUC about sixfold, according to the specification. Bioavailability varied with dose and sex in the reformulation study. The application supports an animal pharmacokinetic case for enhanced delivery; it does not demonstrate a fixed human exposure multiplier, superior human clinical efficacy, or a human safety advantage.

Sources: US Claim 6, Claim 11, and specification Examples 4–6, Tables 5 and 7; international claims.

04 · THE COMMERCIAL QUESTION

A milligram ceiling does not cap absorption

A serving limit controls the amount swallowed. It does not directly control how much reaches systemic circulation, how high blood concentrations peak, or how long exposure lasts. If two permitted preparations contain the same mitragynine amount but one delivers substantially more into the bloodstream, the label ceiling can conceal a meaningful difference in exposure.

That creates a plausible commercial incentive for delivery technology: under an identical milligram cap, an enhanced formulation could offer greater exposure per permitted serving than a conventional preparation. The patent’s rat data illustrate the mechanism. They do not establish consumer preference, a lawful market position, licensing income, or an advantage in humans. Greater exposure can also increase adverse effects; it is not synonymous with a better product.

The category boundary could decide the issue. The patent’s preferred concentrated extract is substantially different from native leaf or a traditional tea. A 45% mitragynine extract cannot simply be presumed to qualify as Category 1. Diluting it with excipients does not establish that its ingredient has a traditional preparation history. Its extraction process, alkaloid composition, chemical integrity, intended use, and safety evidence would matter.

Yet the paper’s conditional discussion of Categories 2 and 3a prevents a categorical conclusion that all extract formulations are excluded. A qualifying extract might seek that pathway if it satisfied the paper’s compositional and evidence conditions. A purified-mitragynine composition or a formulation promoted to treat disease could instead fall on the drug side of the framework. The patent encompasses more than one formulation; no single category can responsibly be assigned to all its embodiments from the dose alone.

The question for policymakers is consequently precise: would the proposed framework admit enhanced-delivery extracts under the same 40 mg ceiling as traditional products, and, if so, what evidence would show comparable exposure and safety? Neither a patent claim nor compliance with one numerical criterion answers that question.

05 · THE DISCLOSURE QUESTION

What readers were told—and what remains unexplained

The policy article’s published conflict-of-interest statement reads:

Author EB was employed by Planted in Science Consulting, LLC. OG and CRM serve as expert witness in kratom-involved legal cases.

The remaining author(s) declared that this work was conducted in the absence of any commercial or financial relationships that could be construed as a potential conflict of interest.

EB is Erin Berthold; OG is Oliver Grundmann; CRM is Christopher R. McCurdy. The statement discloses employment and expert-witness work. It does not identify the formulation application, name the institutional assignee, or describe inventor royalty rights. The separate funding statement says no financial support was received for this work or its publication. That statement does not resolve intellectual-property interests.

The timing makes the question substantive. The international filing was already public in May 2024. The US publication appeared June 4, 2026, before the policy manuscript was received July 1. The manuscript was revised September 9, accepted September 29, and published October 22. The patent interest was not a development that first emerged after the policy paper appeared.

Frontiers’ current conflicts-of-interest policy requires disclosure of actual and potential conflicts at submission and says anything that could be perceived as a potential conflict should be disclosed in the statements section. It expressly encompasses personal, financial, and professional relationships. Under that broad standard, inventor involvement in technology that could be affected by proposed commercial product rules merits a documented relevance assessment.

The public statement is the declaration available for review here. The authors’ underlying submission forms, correspondence with editors, and any editorial determination about this application have not been obtained. Nor has the exact version of the disclosure policy in force at submission been independently archived for this investigation. Those missing records prevent a finding that an author violated a disclosure requirement.

They do not prevent asking why readers were not given this information. Did the authors inform Frontiers? Did an editor decide the veterinary focus or category differences made the interest irrelevant? Was information supplied but left out of the published statement? Do the inventors have prospective royalty rights even if no product has earned money? Institutional assignment does not answer those questions.

The accountability standard: Researchers proposing rules for commercial products should make potentially relevant intellectual-property interests visible enough for readers to assess. Disclosure permits scrutiny of the recommendation’s independence; the absence of proven revenue does not settle the relevance of an invention.
06 · QUESTIONS REQUIRING ANSWERS

The records the authors, university, and journal should explain

The following questions arise from the documents. They have not been sent as interview requests as part of preparing this page; no response or refusal to respond is implied.

  • To McCurdy, Sharma, and Berthold: Did each of you disclose your named-inventor role in this patent family to Frontiers before publication? Please identify the declaration, its date, and the information provided.
  • To Frontiers and the authors: Were patent interests included in submission materials but omitted from the published statement? If so, who made that decision, under which policy, and why were they considered irrelevant?
  • To the University of Florida Research Foundation and the three inventors: Do the inventors have existing or prospective royalty, milestone, equity, or other financial rights connected to this technology? What agreements govern those rights?
  • To the Foundation: Has any part of the technology been licensed, optioned, or commercialized? For which indications and product categories, and under what current patent status?
  • To the policy authors: What independent evidence supports precisely 40 mg per serving and two servings daily? Please provide the derivation, uncertainty, and rationale for applying the same amount to preparations with different pharmacokinetics.
  • To the authors and the Foundation: Would any disclosed patent embodiment qualify under the paper’s proposed Category 2 or 3a pathway? Would enhanced absorption require separate exposure-based limits or safety studies?
  • To McCurdy, Sharma, and Berthold: Were potential commercial interests considered when developing the policy recommendations, and what steps addressed their possible influence?
  • To Frontiers: Will the journal review the patent family against the applicable submission policy and publish its relevance assessment, including any necessary clarification of the conflict statement?

Evidence distinctions. Authorship, named inventorship, published claims, reported experiments, dates, and the text of the published disclosure are documentary facts. The potential effect of a milligram ceiling on enhanced-delivery technology is scientific and regulatory analysis. Private declarations, licensing, inventor compensation, actual market effects, and motive remain unresolved. Patent experiments are reported as application data, not independently replicated findings or demonstrated human clinical outcomes.
