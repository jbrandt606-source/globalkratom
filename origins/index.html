Where Do High-Potency Kratom Products Come From?

Where high-potency kratom-derived products come from, and what patents, laboratory testing, court records and industry documents reveal about the path from Mitragyna speciosa to mitragynine, 7-OH and mitragynine pseudoindoxyl.

How the products trace back upstream

The evidence supports different levels of certainty for each product class.

Product Upstream source What the evidence shows
7-OH Mitragynine Mitragynine can be oxidized to 7-OH. High-potency commercial products show profiles consistent with chemical conversion of mitragynine isolates or whole kratom extracts.1
MP 7-OH / mitragynine-derived material 7-OH is identified as a critical transient precursor to mitragynine pseudoindoxyl. MP was detected in 33 of 35 commercial products tested.1
MGM Compound-specific “MGM” is commercial nomenclature. A precise manufacturing pathway should be assigned only after the specific compound is identified.

The distinction matters: chemical modification changes the finished product. It does not erase the upstream starting material.

Follow the starting material

The finished products may look nothing like a kratom leaf. That does not answer where their chemistry began.

Patents describe the chemical relationships. Commercial product testing identifies the fingerprints of chemical processing. UNODC reports that novel 7-OH products are likely synthesized from purified mitragynine. Industry manufacturing footage documents a process beginning with kratom leaf extract. Taken together, the evidence traces a common upstream chain: Mitragyna speciosa to mitragynine to 7-OH and related downstream alkaloids.

The evidence does not say raw leaf and a finished pseudoindoxyl product are identical. It shows that the modern high-potency market can be built from the chemistry of the kratom plant.

The case in six facts

35 of 35: Mitragynine was detected in every commercial product tested in the 2026 University of Mississippi analysis.

33 of 35: Mitragynine pseudoindoxyl was detected in 33 products, although only two labels disclosed it.

More than 98%: Researchers reported evidence of semisynthetic origin in more than 98% of the 7-OH-labeled products analyzed.

304 products: A separate market study identified 304 commercial 7-OH and pseudoindoxyl products.

UNODC: The United Nations reports that novel commercial 7-OH products are likely synthesized in laboratories from purified mitragynine.

Industrial production: A manufacturer's published account describes cameras following production from kratom leaf extract through oxidation, HPLC analysis, solvent recovery and purification.

The plant supplies the starting molecule

Mitragynine is the principal alkaloid of Mitragyna speciosa. A 2026 Phytochemistry analysis describes 7-OH as an oxygenated analogue present only at trace levels in leaf material and as a critical transient precursor to mitragynine pseudoindoxyl.1

The plant does not need to contain retail-level quantities of 7-OH. It contains the much more abundant upstream molecule, mitragynine.

The upstream supply chain begins with Mitragyna speciosa plant material.

The chemical fingerprint points back to mitragynine

The 2026 University of Mississippi study concluded that elevated 7-OH in concentrated products was likely produced through oxidative chemical conversion of mitragynine isolates or whole kratom extracts. The authors noted that direct extraction of trace 7-OH from chemically unaltered full-spectrum material would not be economically feasible.1

The strongest evidence is the chemical fingerprint. In the 2026 analysis, more than 98 percent of the 7-OH-labeled products examined showed evidence of semisynthetic origin. The researchers did not base that conclusion on the label alone. They identified recurring oxidation and rearrangement products that were not characteristic of authentic leaf extracts. Those byproducts are evidence that the material had undergone chemical processing.1

Mitragynine was detected in all 35 products tested. Mitragynine pseudoindoxyl was detected in 33 of 35, although only two labels identified it.1

The patents show the pathway

A Columbia University patent describes oxidation of mitragynine to 7-hydroxymitragynine and separately describes preparation of mitragynine pseudoindoxyl.4 A later patent concerns production of spiro-oxindole and indoxyl derivatives of mitragynine.5

The patents are evidence of a disclosed chemical route, not proof that every commercial manufacturer follows the same process. Their importance is narrower and stronger: they document that mitragynine can serve as upstream starting material for 7-OH and related mitragynine derivatives, establishing chemical continuity between the plant alkaloid and later high-potency compounds.

Industry footage shows the process at commercial scale

An industry account of Hamilton Morris's 2026 documentary says cameras followed production from kratom leaf extract through oxidation in a 2,000-liter vessel, HPLC analysis, solvent recovery and liquid-liquid purification.6

This is direct industry evidence of scale. Unlike a patent, which shows what can be done, the footage documents a commercial facility beginning with kratom leaf extract and following it through oxidation, analytical testing, solvent recovery and purification. It does not establish that every producer uses the same process, but it shows that the leaf-to-7-OH pathway exists outside the laboratory literature.

The market is already large

Hill, Boyer, Grundmann and Smith identified 304 semi-synthetic 7-OH and mitragynine pseudoindoxyl products marketed online. Of those, 82.2 percent were 7-OH-only products, 14.5 percent combined 7-OH and pseudoindoxyl, and 3.3 percent were pseudoindoxyl-only. Chewable or sublingual tablets were the most common format, followed by shots.2

The researchers concluded that the difficulty and expense of isolating 7-OH from kratom leaves, together with the amount of active ingredient being sold, strongly suggested laboratory manufacture from purified mitragynine.2

UNODC describes the same chain

In August 2025, the United Nations Office on Drugs and Crime reported that novel high-concentration 7-OH and mitragynine pseudoindoxyl products had emerged particularly in the United States. UNODC noted that 7-OH is a minor constituent of kratom, is also a metabolite of mitragynine, and is further metabolized to mitragynine pseudoindoxyl.3

UNODC summarized testing of six products marketed as 7-OH. All contained 7-OH together with varying amounts of mitragynine pseudoindoxyl, mitragynine and paynantheine, and the report stated that these novel products were likely synthesized from purified mitragynine.3

Court records connect the chemistry to commerce

In Missouri litigation, the Attorney General alleged that RRR Trading commissioned the manufacture of products containing mitragynine, 7-OH, MGM-15 and other psychoactive alkaloids, including pseudoindoxyl, and sold them through direct and retail channels.7

Those allegations are not findings of fact. Their value to the supply-chain analysis is that they describe the commercial step after the chemistry: commissioning products containing named kratom-related alkaloids and moving those finished products into distribution and retail channels.

Some labels say where the alkaloid came from

An exhibit filed with the Sanders affidavit shows a chewable product declaring 7.5 mg of 7-hydroxymitragynine per tablet and identifying it as "from Mitragyna Speciosa Aerial Parts." The same label warns that the product contains psychoactive alkaloids that may include mitragynine, 7-hydroxymitragynine and mitragynine pseudoindoxyl.8

The significance is narrow but direct: at least one commercial label expressly connects a measured high-potency 7-OH product to Mitragyna speciosa.

"Synthetic" does not answer the origin question

Calling a finished compound synthetic or semi-synthetic can describe how it was produced. It does not necessarily identify where the manufacturing chain began.

If mitragynine is isolated from Mitragyna speciosa and chemically converted into 7-OH, the resulting product has undergone chemical transformation. If 7-OH is subsequently converted or rearranged into another compound, another transformation has occurred. The upstream feedstock can still trace back to kratom-derived mitragynine.

The chemistry can change. The supply chain does not vanish.

What "immediate precursor" actually means

Chemical precursor is not the same thing as a federally designated immediate precursor.

Under the Controlled Substances Act, "immediate precursor" is a legal term. Federal law requires findings concerning whether a substance is the principal compound used or produced primarily for use in manufacturing a controlled substance, whether it is an immediate chemical intermediary, and whether controlling it is necessary to prevent, curtail or limit manufacture of the controlled substance.9

The evidence collected here establishes chemical and manufacturing relationships. It does not, by itself, make mitragynine or 7-OH a legally designated immediate precursor. That designation is a regulatory determination.

What the evidence does establish is the factual basis for asking the question: mitragynine sits directly upstream of manufactured 7-OH, and peer-reviewed literature describes 7-OH as a critical transient precursor to mitragynine pseudoindoxyl.1

What the evidence actually establishes

No single document proves the entire supply chain. Taken together, the sources answer different parts of the same question.

  • Botanical and analytical research establishes that mitragynine is the dominant upstream kratom alkaloid and that naturally occurring 7-OH is present only at trace levels.
  • Commercial product testing finds concentrations and chemical byproducts inconsistent with simple, unmodified leaf extraction and consistent with semisynthetic processing.
  • Patents document chemical pathways linking mitragynine, 7-OH and mitragynine pseudoindoxyl.
  • Industry footage documents commercial-scale processing that begins with kratom leaf extract and proceeds through oxidation and purification.
  • Market research and UNODC surveillance show that hundreds of 7-OH and pseudoindoxyl products have reached consumers and identify purified mitragynine as the likely manufacturing source for high-concentration 7-OH products.
  • Product labeling and court records connect the chemistry to named finished products and commercial distribution. Court allegations are identified as allegations.

The strength of the evidence is cumulative. The plant supplies the abundant upstream alkaloid. The chemistry literature shows the conversion pathway. Product testing detects fingerprints of that processing. Industry and court records show those compounds moving into commercial production and retail products.

The supply chain in one sentence

The plant supplies mitragynine. Mitragynine supplies a route to 7-OH. 7-OH supplies a route to pseudoindoxyl and related compounds. Manufacturing turns those compounds into retail products.

Powder, extract, tablet and pseudoindoxyl are not the same product. That is precisely the point. The modern market has learned to take a botanical alkaloid and move it through increasingly sophisticated stages of extraction, conversion, purification and formulation.

The finished product may be new. The chemical family tree is not.
