10-Second Summary

  • Ohio commenters in policing, fire/EMS, healthcare, trucking and hazardous work described using kratom while remaining on the job.
  • Manufacturer-side court materials discuss seizures, psychosis, sedation, hallucinations, coma, cardiovascular effects, organ toxicity and possible effects on cognition and response time.
  • Happy Hippo’s own Terms warn about opioid-receptor activity, dependence and severe opioid-like withdrawal.
  • The military prohibits kratom for service members. States can build similar fitness-for-duty protections for private-sector safety-sensitive jobs — including nurses.

Important: A worker’s statement that they use kratom does not prove that worker was impaired. The question is whether safety-sensitive workplaces should have a way to evaluate a psychoactive substance that may fall outside routine testing.

What the Manufacturer-Side Science Says

In federal litigation involving Botanic Tonics, expert Robert Verkerk, PhD, described kratom as having a “complex toxicological and pharmacodynamic profile.” His court-submitted report associates long-term or high-dose use with anxiety, irritability, aggression, tremors, seizures and psychosis, and discusses clinical reports involving respiratory distress and hepatotoxicity.

Cardiopulmonary & Organ Effects

  • Tachycardia / palpitations
  • Cardiac arrest in reviewed reports

  • Liver injury / hepatotoxicity
  • Possible kidney impairment at high doses
  • Dependence & withdrawal

A product-specific scientific statement submitted for Botanic Tonics also acknowledged that kratom and kava can affect the central nervous system, with potential high-dose effects on cognition and response time. In the Ohio litigation, plaintiff-side testimony distinguished traditional leaf from concentrated products and described concentrated kratom as becoming “more like morphine.”

The Seller Warnings Are Not Subtle

Happy Hippo’s August 26, 2026 Terms of Service say kratom interacts with opioid receptors, may be habit-forming, may lead to dependence, and that misuse may result in opioid-like withdrawal symptoms and other adverse effects. Its safety section says some users have reported severe opioid-like withdrawal after stopping and instructs consumers to seek immediate medical attention or emergency care for adverse reactions.

A substance can be legal to buy and still raise a separate question: is it compatible with a job where an error can injure a patient, passenger, coworker or member of the public?

The Military Already Treats Kratom as a Readiness Issue

The Department of Defense has placed kratom on its prohibited-substances list for service members. Air Force guidance says members of the armed forces should not purchase or consume products containing kratom. Navy/Marine Corps guidance prohibits the use, possession, distribution and manufacture of kratom, mitragynine and 7-hydroxymitragynine products. Army guidance likewise prohibits service-member use.

That policy distinction matters: a substance does not have to appear on a routine drug screen for an organization to decide it is incompatible with safety-sensitive duty.

Official sources: U.S. Air Force · ALNAV 003/26 · Army CID

What States Can Do — Including for Private-Sector Nurses

States do not have to limit workplace protections to state employees or DOT-regulated drivers. They can define safety-sensitive work by the risk created by impairment — regardless of whether the employer is a state agency, private hospital, nursing home, contractor, warehouse or utility.

1. Define the job, not the employer

Cover roles where impairment could cause serious injury: direct patient care, EMS, policing, heavy equipment, hazardous processes and professional driving.

2. Authorize targeted testing

Allow designated safety-sensitive employers to use mitragynine/kratom testing for reasonable suspicion, post-incident, return-to-duty and appropriately defined safety-sensitive programs.

3. Build in due process

Require confirmatory laboratory testing, medical review, documented policy and a fair process before discipline or permanent removal.

For a nurse, the question is not whether the hospital is public or private. The question is whether the person administering medications, assessing patients and responding to emergencies is fit for duty right now.

The goal need not be policing lawful off-duty conduct. It can be narrower: preventing a worker from performing hazardous duties when there is objective reason to question fitness for duty.

Drug-Free Is Not the Same as Fit for Duty

A conventional workplace drug screen only identifies the substances it is designed to detect. If an emerging psychoactive substance is not on the panel, “passed the drug test” does not necessarily answer the occupational-safety question.

Detection is not automatically proof of impairment. But “I feel fine” is not an objective safety system either.

A drug-free workplace tests for the drugs on its list. A fit-for-duty workplace asks the question that matters most when other people’s safety is on the line: can this person safely perform this job right now?
