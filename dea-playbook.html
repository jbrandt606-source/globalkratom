Did DEA Borrow the Kratom Industry's Playbook?

How the federal 7-OH scheduling plan mirrors the regulatory strategy advanced by Pinney Associates

The Remarkable Overlap

The Drug Enforcement Administration's new notice of intent to place 7-hydroxymitragynine above a specified threshold into Schedule I is being presented as a federal crackdown on an emerging opioid threat.

And it is.

But it is also something else: a remarkably precise execution of the regulatory strategy that Pinney Associates laid out months earlier on behalf of interests connected to the American Kratom Association.

The similarity is not merely that both documents conclude that concentrated 7-OH should be scheduled. Scientists, regulators, poison centers and clinicians have increasingly recognized the dangers posed by commercially manufactured 7-OH products.

The deeper similarity is in the architecture of the argument:

  • Separate 7-OH from kratom.
  • Schedule the dangerous derivative.
  • Preserve mitragynine and the commercial kratom market.

That is the policy line Pinney Associates recommended to the Ohio Board of Pharmacy in January 2026. It is now, in substantial part, the line adopted by DEA.

There is no proof from these documents alone that DEA copied Pinney Associates. DEA states that its threshold was adapted from the Department of Health and Human Services' July 2025 scientific evaluation. But the overlap raises an important question: Did federal regulators independently reach the same narrow solution, or did an industry-backed framing become the governing federal framework?

The Pinney Associates Proposal

The Pinney Associates report was developed for the Ohio Board of Pharmacy by Jack Henningfield and several coauthors. Its disclosure section states that the report was funded by the Center for Plant Science and Health, a nonprofit established by the American Kratom Association. It also states that Pinney Associates consults for the Center, the American Kratom Association and developers and marketers of kratom products. Henningfield is also identified as a paid expert witness in kratom litigation. Pinney associates 2026.pdf

Its core recommendation could not be clearer:

"Natural kratom leaf products and extracts, including natural mitragynine products, do not warrant CSA scheduling."

The report then recommends that:

"7-OH, whether naturally occurring or synthesized, does warrant CSA scheduling."

It further argues that synthetic compounds derived from kratom or mitragynine may deserve control, while natural kratom, mitragynine products and regulated extracts should remain available. Pinney associates 2026.pdf

That is the entire industry-preservation strategy in three steps:

  1. Define 7-OH as a distinct opioid product.
  2. Remove it from the market through Schedule I.
  3. Use that distinction to protect mitragynine and commercial kratom products from broader scheduling.

DEA has now adopted nearly that same boundary.

DEA Schedules the Bad Molecule, Not the Parent Market

DEA's notice does not propose scheduling kratom, mitragynine or all mitragynine-related substances.

It targets only 7-hydroxymitragynine above a specified threshold.

For botanical kratom material, DEA proposes a cutoff of more than 0.050 percent 7-OH by dry weight. For synthetic products, processed extracts, concentrates, edibles and pressed pills, the rule generally applies above 0.050 percent or when the article contains more than 1 milligram of 7-OH. DEA intent to schedule.pdf

That structure does more than remove high-dose 7-OH tablets from smoke shops. It creates a legally protected category of kratom products that fall below the threshold.

In other words, the proposed order does not merely identify what DEA intends to prohibit. It also establishes what remains outside the federal scheduling action.

That is precisely the distinction Pinney Associates urged regulators to make.

Parallel One: 7-OH is not kratom

Pinney Associates repeatedly characterizes the difference between concentrated 7-OH and natural kratom as fundamental. The report endorses the federal description of the distinction as "night and day in terms of the public health risk." It describes concentrated 7-OH as a new class of high-potency opioid products, while portraying leaf and mitragynine products as historically used, pharmacologically milder and more appropriate for regulation than prohibition. Pinney associates 2026.pdf

DEA uses more cautious language, but the conceptual distinction is nearly identical.

DEA acknowledges that 7-OH may occur naturally, may be synthesized from mitragynine and is also produced in the body as a metabolite of mitragynine. It then states that concentrated or isolated formulations deliver "unattenuated, high-potency effects," while the raw plant matrix may produce a modified or attenuated physiological effect because of competing alkaloids. DEA intent to schedule.pdf

That is an important concession.

DEA is not saying the molecule becomes chemically different when it comes from kratom. In fact, the agency explicitly states that natural and synthetic 7-OH are structurally identical and possess the same intrinsic receptor activity.

Instead, DEA adopts the industry-friendly distinction at the product level: low-level 7-OH inside a botanical matrix is treated differently from concentrated 7-OH delivered directly.

Pinney Associates makes the same move.

Parallel Two: A Morphine-Like Opioid with Abuse Potential

Both documents characterize 7-OH as an opioid with pharmacological effects resembling morphine.

Pinney Associates concludes that 7-OH has substantial mu-opioid receptor activity, morphine-like reinforcing effects, opioid-type reward, dependence liability and the potential to cause respiratory depression. It argues that this profile supports treatment of 7-OH as an opioid for Controlled Substances Act purposes. Pinney associates 2026.pdf

DEA similarly states that 7-OH has opioidergic activity comparable to Schedule II opioids such as morphine. Its notice cites risks including tolerance, dependence, addiction, respiratory depression and death. It also describes 7-OH as having abuse potential similar to Schedule I and II opioids, including heroin, morphine and fentanyl. DEA intent to schedule.pdf

The agreement is substantial:

  • Both identify mu-opioid receptor activity.
  • Both cite animal self-administration and reward evidence.
  • Both cite morphine-like effects.
  • Both identify dependence and withdrawal.
  • Both accept respiratory depression as a relevant risk.
  • Both conclude that 7-OH warrants controlled-substance scheduling.

The policy dispute is therefore not whether 7-OH is an opioid threat. Pinney Associates and DEA agree that it is.

The dispute is where the danger ends.

Parallel Three: The Recent Market Transformation

Both documents tell essentially the same origin story.

Traditional kratom existed for years. Then companies began manufacturing concentrated 7-OH products in tablets, gummies, shots, powders, strips and other rapid-delivery forms. These products were sold through smoke shops, gas stations and the internet, often under the appearance of kratom or a botanical supplement.

Pinney Associates describes a "pivotal shift" beginning around 2022, when concentrated products containing chemically elevated 7-OH entered the market. It says these products contain levels hundreds of times greater than those expected in natural leaf and created a new class of products marketed under the guise of kratom. Pinney associates 2026.pdf

DEA likewise describes an aggressive national market for tablets, shots, gummies, powders, films, pouches, nasal sprays and liquid extracts. It emphasizes highly variable doses, colorful packaging, misleading "natural extract" claims and sales through gas stations, smoke shops and online vendors. DEA intent to schedule.pdf

Even the data sources substantially overlap. Both rely on research involving product marketing, chemical analysis, social-media discussions, poison-center reports, adverse-event reporting and reports of dependence and withdrawal.

Parallel Four: Use the Temporary-Scheduling Pathway

Pinney Associates did not merely recommend control. It identified the legal mechanism.

Its report contains a separate discussion of temporary scheduling under 21 U.S.C. § 811(h). It explains that emergency scheduling requires consideration of three factors:

  • history and current pattern of abuse;
  • scope, duration and significance of abuse; and
  • risk to public health.

The report then concludes that 7-OH is the one kratom-related alkaloid with a sufficient evidentiary basis for emergency scheduling. Pinney associates 2026.pdf

DEA's notice follows that exact statutory path.

It invokes § 811(h), organizes its substantive case around Factors 4, 5 and 6, and concludes that temporary Schedule I placement is necessary to avoid an imminent hazard to public safety. DEA intent to schedule.pdf

Of course, those are the factors Congress requires DEA to consider, so structural similarity alone is not evidence of copying.

But Pinney Associates did more than describe the statute. It recommended this exact outcome: emergency Schedule I treatment for 7-OH, without extending that treatment to mitragynine or kratom.

DEA has now selected that outcome.

Parallel Five: The Threshold Protects Ordinary Kratom Products

The threshold may be the most consequential similarity.

Pinney Associates argues that regulators should distinguish naturally low levels of 7-OH from products containing boosted or concentrated amounts. It cites approximately 0.05 percent as a dividing line associated with ordinary products and argues that products with artificially elevated 7-OH should be treated differently from leaf and mitragynine extracts. Pinney associates 2026.pdf

DEA's proposed definition uses more than 0.050 percent for botanical material and processed products, with an additional 1-milligram trigger for alternative articles. DEA intent to schedule.pdf

The practical effect is enormous.

The rule does not prohibit 7-OH as an absolute chemical matter. It prohibits 7-OH above a commercially and legally significant threshold.

That means the federal government is accepting the proposition that some amount of a Schedule I-worthy opioid may remain in lawful kratom material, provided the product stays below the designated line.

This is not how the public normally understands Schedule I. It is a product-carveout strategy built around concentration.

And it aligns closely with the position long advanced by the regulated-kratom industry: target "enhanced" 7-OH while preserving products that meet a defined alkaloid standard.

Parallel Six: Preserve Regulation as the Alternative to Prohibition

Pinney Associates promotes state consumer-protection laws, manufacturing standards, labeling rules, age restrictions and product registration as the proper approach for ordinary kratom.

DEA does not formally endorse the Kratom Consumer Protection Act. But the notice discusses those state laws at length, identifies 19 states that have adopted versions of them and notes that several states impose limits on 7-OH concentration. DEA intent to schedule.pdf

The resulting division is familiar:

  • Concentrated 7-OH: Schedule I.
  • Low-7-OH kratom: remain in commerce under state product regulation.
  • Mitragynine: not included in the federal order.
  • Broader kratom marketplace: preserved.

That is substantially the regulatory ecosystem sought by Pinney Associates and the American Kratom Association.

But the DEA Document Is Not Simply Pinney Associates with a Federal Seal

The similarities should not obscure important differences.

Pinney Associates spends substantial space arguing that natural kratom offers public-health benefits, that users may rely on it for pain or substance-use self-management and that prohibition could drive people toward fentanyl, heroin or illicit products. It repeatedly portrays kratom as comparatively benign and emphasizes the potential harm of criminalizing consumers. Pinney associates 2026.pdf

DEA does not adopt those claims.

DEA does not declare kratom safe. It does not find that kratom treats opioid withdrawal. It does not endorse kratom as harm reduction. It does not approve mitragynine as a dietary ingredient or medicine.

DEA instead says that its current action is directed at 7-OH above a specified threshold.

That distinction matters. An agency decision not to schedule mitragynine in this particular order is not the same as a finding that mitragynine is safe, beneficial, lawful under the Food, Drug, and Cosmetic Act or unsuitable for future control.

The DEA notice also contains newer and more extensive evidence than the January Pinney report, including:

  • 86 FAERS cases involving 7-OH, 79 classified as serious and nine reporting death;
  • 85 DEA TOX cases, including 55 fatal cases;
  • 42 NFLIS reports from 12 states in 2025;
  • poison-center exposure data through July 2025;
  • state health alerts and newer clinical case reports. DEA intent to schedule.pdf

So the federal action is not merely a reproduction of an industry report. It has its own administrative record and relies on HHS, FDA, DEA surveillance and statutory findings.

The question is not whether the two documents are identical.

They are not.

The question is why the final federal policy boundary so closely matches the one proposed by consultants with disclosed ties to the kratom industry.

The Strategic Victory Hidden Inside the Crackdown

The kratom industry will publicly condemn the scheduling of 7-OH, especially companies that profit from concentrated tablets and extracts.

But for the traditional kratom lobby, this may also represent a major strategic victory.

For years, the industry has faced the possibility that federal regulators might schedule mitragynine, 7-OH and potentially the plant itself. A federal action limited to concentrated 7-OH creates a powerful new talking point:

DEA reviewed the issue and chose not to schedule kratom or mitragynine.

That statement will undoubtedly be repeated in statehouses, courtrooms, lobbying materials and future regulatory hearings.

It will be used to argue that leaf kratom has been federally distinguished from dangerous opioid products.

But that argument goes further than the notice itself.

DEA did not issue an order declaring kratom safe. It issued an order addressing one defined substance above one specified threshold.

Still, the commercial value of that distinction is difficult to overstate.

By drawing a line around 7-OH, the government may simultaneously remove one dangerous product category and strengthen the political position of the remaining kratom market.

The Question Regulators Should Ask

The narrow scheduling of concentrated 7-OH may be scientifically defensible. The evidence of opioid-like effects, dependence, withdrawal, respiratory depression, poison-center cases and serious adverse outcomes is substantial.

But regulators should be candid about the second-order effect.

This is not merely a ban on concentrated 7-OH.

It is also a federal decision, at least for now, to leave mitragynine and threshold-compliant kratom products outside the scheduling order.

That result closely tracks the policy framework advanced by Pinney Associates in a report funded by an organization established by the American Kratom Association and prepared by consultants with disclosed relationships to kratom interests.

That does not establish plagiarism. It does not prove improper influence. And it does not negate the evidence supporting control of 7-OH.

It does, however, justify scrutiny.

When an industry-funded report says, "Schedule this molecule, preserve our market," and the federal government later draws almost the same line, the public deserves to know how that boundary was chosen, who advocated for it and whether competing regulatory options were seriously considered.

Because the biggest story may not be that DEA scheduled 7-OH.

It may be that, in doing so, DEA adopted almost exactly the solution the kratom industry needed.

Call It Pinney's Plan, DEA's Policy

The DEA's notice of intent to schedule 7-hydroxymitragynine above a specified threshold is a significant public health action. The evidence of harm from concentrated 7-OH products is real and growing.

But the policy architecture that DEA has adopted—schedule the dangerous derivative, preserve the parent market—was not invented by the agency. It was the precise recommendation of Pinney Associates in a report funded by the American Kratom Association's nonprofit arm.

Whether that convergence is coincidence, convergence of evidence or successful industry influence is a question that deserves an answer.

What is not in question is that the federal government has now drawn a line that the kratom industry has long sought: a line that says 7-OH is the problem, not kratom itself.

That line may be good policy. Or it may be a dangerous half-measure that leaves the broader kratom market untouched while the next wave of potent derivatives emerges.

Either way, the public should understand how that line came to be drawn.
