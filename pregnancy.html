Pregnancy & Neonatal Effects

A national poison center study of neonatal opioid withdrawal

James B. Leonard and Colleen P. Cowdery, Washington Poison Center, examined U.S. National Poison Data System reports involving infants younger than 21 days from 2015 through 2023.

20Exposure reports identified
14Included in analysis
8 daysMedian symptom duration
64.3%Received opioid therapy (9 of 14)

Reported manifestations included tremor, agitation, increased muscle tone, rapid breathing, reduced feeding and weight loss. Treatment commonly involved morphine or methadone.

Why this matters: The study period precedes widespread availability of concentrated 7-OH products. Neonatal withdrawal was already being reported before that market emerged. Product composition was not established, so the study cannot assign every case to plain leaf or quantify risk by formulation.

Retrospective poison center reports are not a population incidence estimate. Overlap with published case reports is possible.

Key findings

  • Human reports associate prenatal kratom exposure with neonatal opioid withdrawal syndrome (NOWS), a form of neonatal abstinence syndrome (NAS).
  • Some affected infants require opioid treatment and extended hospital care.
  • Routine drug screens may not detect kratom alkaloids.
  • Neither a safe prenatal dose nor long-term developmental safety has been established.

What happens at delivery?

Mitragynine acts at opioid receptors and can be metabolized to 7-hydroxymitragynine (7-OH). Repeated prenatal exposure can plausibly produce fetal physical dependence, followed by withdrawal after birth.

Conceptual mechanism, not a measured human placental pharmacokinetic model. Transfer rates and fetal dose-response relationships remain uncertain.

Breastfeeding and lactation

NIH LactMed (updated December 15, 2025) describes limited information about breastfeeding during kratom use. Infant exposure and safety are not adequately characterized. Nursing patients should generally avoid kratom and obtain individualized advice if dependence or neonatal withdrawal is present. Breastfeeding is not a substitute for indicated neonatal treatment.

Lead contamination: an additional prenatal risk

Lead crosses the placenta

Lead exposure can harm fetal development; no safe exposure level has been established. Commercial kratom samples have been found to contain lead. The presence of lead in selected samples does not establish exposure in every neonatal case.
