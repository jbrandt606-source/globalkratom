REGULATORY ACCOUNTABILITY · EDITORIAL ANALYSIS · NOVEMBER 16, 2026

The credentials are real.
So are the clients.

Jack E. Henningfield is not a random commentator with an opinion about kratom. Pinney Associates identifies him as its vice president for research, health policy, and abuse liability, with a NIDA background, a Johns Hopkins adjunct appointment, and a practice spanning abuse-potential assessments and regulatory strategy. [1]

That résumé deserves scrutiny, not genuflection. The relevant question is not whether he knows the science. It is whose assignment he is carrying out, which evidence supports the conclusion, and what the introduction leaves for the footnotes.

The records below document kratom advocacy work, manufacturer-funded vaping research, JUUL defense testimony, and commercial cannabinoid-development connections. Those are different assignments—not interchangeable accusations. [2][6][14][19][20]

A distinguished résumé is not a conflict-of-interest waiver.

WHY HE MATTERS TO THE KRATOM INDUSTRY

The AKA does not need another salesman.
It needs a scientist at the microphone.

Here is the documented role behind that distinction. His 2022 review states that the American Kratom Association’s legal regulatory counsel commissioned Henningfield and his Pinney colleagues to prepare an eight-factor analysis for submission to DEA in 2016. The later review recommended against controlled-substance scheduling. [2]

The regulatory argument

His work supplies a technical case against scheduling—not merely a general interest in kratom. The 2022 article’s disclosure identifies advice to the AKA and its affiliated Center for Plant Science and Health. [2]

The legislative argument

In January 2022 Ohio testimony, he identified himself as an AKA adviser and recommended continued adult access under a regulatory framework. That puts the scientific argument directly before lawmakers. [3]

The courtroom argument

A 2025/2026 publication discloses that he prepared reports and gave depositions for several defendants in kratom litigation, addressing addictiveness, risks, benefits, and regulation. [4]

Our assessment: that combination makes him valuable to the industry. He can translate a position about continued access into the language of pharmacology, regulation, and expert evidence. A trade association’s argument becomes more persuasive when it arrives with an academic biography.

Keep the funding record straight. The 2022 review says the AKA supported an earlier summary, not preparation of that journal article; it also says clients had no input into the assessment or conclusions. A disclosed advisory relationship is not proof that every publication was commissioned. [2]

The introduction should not stop at Johns Hopkins.

THE LEGISLATIVE VERSION

Coffee-family theater is not pharmacology.

His January 25, 2022 Ohio testimony presented this as a basic fact: [3]

“Kratom is in the coffee family, not the opium poppy family – it is not an opioid.”

Jack E. Henningfield · Ohio House Health Committee · January 25, 2022

His own review discusses mitragynine’s activity at mu-opioid receptors. That does not make kratom identical to fentanyl. It does mean that a botanical family tree cannot settle a pharmacological question. [2]

The testimony also acknowledged possible dependence and the absence of FDA approval for therapeutic use. Those qualifications belong beside the reassuring botanical comparison—not beneath its rhetorical shadow. [3]

A plant’s relatives are not its safety data.

072017–2019 · Cannabinoid strategy

Less restrictive scheduling was on the program.

The 2017 ICRS abstract “Beyond Schedule I or II…” lists Henningfield and four Pinney colleagues. It explicitly connects cannabinoid scheduling with patient access and commercial viability, and discusses support for less restrictive schedules. [16]

In 2018, he and Daniel Wang presented “Cannabinoid Abuse Potential Assessment: The 8-Factor Analysis as an Opportunity” to an audience including regulatory and commercial professionals. [17]

A 2019 webinar with Sidney Schnoll and Marilyn Huestis covered cannabis-based drug development and scheduling, while acknowledging unresolved safety questions. These programs do not identify a particular paying cannabis client. [18]

This was regulatory strategy for developers, not a seminar on gardening.

08March 2, 2023 · MyMD / Supera-CBD

The cannabinoid connection has a company name.

MyMD Pharmaceuticals announced that DEA had determined its investigational Supera-CBD was not then a controlled substance or listed chemical. Its SEC-filed announcement quoted Henningfield: [19]

“It is a tremendous benefit to be able to conduct drug development without the burden of dealing with a scheduled product.”

He also described continuing support for MyMD’s development program. Supera-CBD was a synthetic CBD analog, not marijuana flower. The record is a company announcement—not the DEA letter, proof of his fee, or proof he authored the submission. [19]

Commercial cannabinoid work. No insinuation necessary.

09June 2026 · Harmony Biosciences

The CBD poster came with a sponsor.

Pinney lists Henningfield as a coauthor of a conference presentation on real-world CBD outcomes. Its funding statement says: [20]

“This study was funded by Harmony Biosciences Management.”

The public summary interprets declining adverse outcomes after 2022 as supporting continued CBD therapeutic development and nonscheduled status. This is company-funded cannabinoid research with an explicit scheduling implication. [20]

The listing establishes a conference presentation, not a separately verified journal publication or a personal payment to Henningfield.

The sponsor belongs in the introduction, not an archaeological expedition.

102025/2026 · Abuse-potential methods

He also argues about the rules of the test.

In a commentary published online in November 2025 and in the January 2026 issue, Henningfield and coauthors argue that standard abuse-potential methods may need modification for some novel drugs, psychedelics, and cannabinoids. They discuss the risk of overly restrictive scheduling as well as insufficient control. [4]

That is a methodological position—not evidence of misconduct. But a proposal to revise an assessment method must earn its authority through validation, not the inconvenience of the regulatory outcome.

A test does not become defective because the answer is commercially awkward.

The point is not that he lacks expertise.

His marijuana research reaches back to a 1986 clinical study of subjective and smoking effects. In 1992, he coauthored two Blood Cannabinoids papers with Marilyn Huestis and Edward Cone, on THC absorption and estimating time since exposure. Those earlier papers establish experience—not, on this record, commercial marijuana sponsorship. [21][22][23]

His 2018 vaping commentary argued that policymakers should consider the possible harm of failing to use lower-risk substitutes for smoking. Pinney describes its JUUL work in that harm-reduction framework and says it does not work on combustible products. That is its stated rationale; it is not an answer to every question about funding, methods, or public-health tradeoffs. [24][7]

Expertise explains why a consultant is valuable. It does not make the client disappear.

EDITORIAL ASSESSMENT

Different products. A familiar case for keeping access open.

Across these records, the recurring argument is recognizable: assess relative abuse potential, emphasize possible benefits, and warn that restrictive policy can create harms of its own. Those can be legitimate scientific questions. They can also be commercially valuable arguments. The two possibilities are not mutually exclusive.

The answer is not to declare every funded study worthless. It is to stop treating academic prestige as a substitute for independent examination. Ask what the study measured, which population it studied, what it left untested, and whether the public claim outruns the actual result.

Calling a report independent does not settle its independence. Publishing a disclosure does not make the relationship irrelevant. Comparing a product with something worse does not establish that it is safe.

The industry is entitled to an expert.
The public is entitled to the rest of the introduction.

The record is strong enough without inventing a scandal.

What is documented

Named consulting relationships; manufacturer funding of identified research; cannabinoid-development support; disclosed kratom-litigation work; and JUUL expert retention confirmed in court records. The citations distinguish Henningfield’s personal role from his firm’s activities. [5][6][19][4][14]

What is not established here

His compensation amounts, complete contracts, an undisclosed payment arrangement, a named recreational-marijuana retailer client, or fabrication of research. Pharmaceutical cannabinoid development is not the same thing as selling recreational cannabis. A limited court ruling is not a misconduct finding.

A client list is not a rebuttal to a study.
It is an excellent reason to read past the résumé.

FOR LAWMAKERS, REPORTERS, AND REGULATORS

Four questions that belong before the applause.

  1. Who retained you for this particular assignment? Identify the client, funding source, scope, and whether the work is research, regulatory consulting, advocacy, or litigation support.
  2. What did the evidence actually measure? Acute drug liking, long-term dependence, withdrawal, population harm, and successful substitution are different questions. Which one does this study answer?
  3. Which conclusions survived independent scrutiny? Ask for the full methods, prespecified outcomes, appropriate comparators, limitations, and independent replication—not just the reassuring summary.
  4. What evidence would change the recommendation? A scientifically defensible position should identify the findings that would cause its author to revise it.

Respect the credentials. Examine the assignment.
Nobody gets to submit a biography in place of an answer.
